# Part I. A Guide for Advocates and Planners

<!-- section: guide-why-this-report-exists -->
## Why this report exists

Alabama's mental health system is not one system. It is a set of systems — a state agency and its contracted community centers, Medicaid and commercial insurers, hospitals and emergency departments, schools, courts and jails, housing coalitions, veterans' services, and nonprofits and faith communities — that together determine whether a person in Alabama can find help, afford it, reach it, and keep it. No single office sees all of it. This report is an attempt to describe all of it in one place, in plain language, so that people who want to improve it can see where they stand. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0012 -->

The report has three uses. For **advocates**, it supplies verified, dated figures and the name of the body responsible for each part of the system, so that a request to a legislator, a county commission or a foundation can be specific. For **planners** in nonprofits, providers and coalitions, it offers a common structure for deciding where to invest and for recognizing which partner owns the adjacent piece. For **the coalition itself**, it gives a baseline against which later editions — and a planned North Alabama regional edition built on the same template — can measure change. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0013 -->

<!-- section: guide-how-the-system-is-described -->
## How the system is described

The analysis uses eight domains adapted from the World Health Organization's six "building blocks" of a health system, plus two that this report adds, on equity and on community engagement [1]: <!-- b: B0015 -->

1. **Governance, policy and legal foundations** — who decides, under what law, with what accountability. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0016 -->

2. **Financing and resource allocation** — where the money comes from and how it moves. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0017 -->

3. **Service-delivery continuum** — what services exist, where, for whom. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0018 -->

4. **Workforce development and well-being** — who delivers care and whether there are enough of them. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0019 -->

5. **Medicines, technology and infrastructure** — buildings, beds, data pipes, telehealth, medications. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0020 -->

6. **Information, data and quality improvement** — how the system knows what it is doing. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0021 -->

7. **Equity, inclusion and cultural responsiveness** — who the system reaches and who it misses. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0022 -->

8. **Community engagement, education and advocacy** — how the public shapes the system. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0023 -->

Two lenses run through the report. The **continuum-of-care lens** asks, at each point, whether Alabama has promotion and prevention, outpatient treatment, crisis response, inpatient and forensic care, and recovery supports — a range drawn from the National Academies' spectrum of promotion, prevention, treatment and maintenance and from the state mental health directors' association's argument that a full continuum of psychiatric care is necessary [2] [3]. The **justice lens** uses the Sequential Intercept Model's six intercepts, from community services through community corrections [4] [5], because in Alabama the justice system is a regular point of contact for people with mental illness [6]. <!-- b: B0024 -->

Four sections in Part III describe systems that lie outside the Department of Mental Health: children, youth and families (using the national System of Care principles) [7]; the justice interface; housing and social determinants (using the Healthy People 2030 framework) [8]; and payers, including the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic transition that is reshaping how community care is funded. <!-- b: B0025 -->

<!-- section: guide-what-the-evidence-shows-in-brief -->
## What the evidence shows, in brief

The findings below are the ones a reader should carry away. Each is developed in its section. Figures are for state fiscal year 2025 (October 2024–September 2025) unless noted; bracketed numbers point to the source list, and on this site each one opens a summary of the source and the passage that supports the sentence. Three terms recur: **988** is the national suicide and crisis line, answered in Alabama by four in-state call centers [9]; a **CCBHC** (Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic) is a community clinic paid a daily Medicaid rate to provide a defined set of services including 24/7 crisis response [10]; and **_Hunter_** is the federal consent decree governing how long people may wait in jail for competency evaluation and restoration [11]. <!-- b: B0027 -->

- **Crisis response has been Alabama's major structural gain since 2020, and it is now under scrutiny.** Six regional crisis centers, four in-state 988 call centers and fourteen mobile crisis teams now operate [12] [9] [13]; the first crisis center opened in 2021 and 988 launched in July 2022 [14] [15]. In FY2025 the crisis centers performed 9,929 evaluations and the 988 call centers handled 40,053 calls, with an in-state answer rate of 87.4 percent for the year that reached 90.9 percent in May 2025 [16]. The FY2027 budget, as reported, withholds the last two quarterly installments of the $42 million for crisis diversion and rural crisis-care centers until the Department "reports the status of the programs" to the budget chairs, the Finance Director and the Legislative Fiscal Officer [17] [18]; the Senate committee's version had tied third- and fourth-quarter payments for the six crisis centers to reporting of costs, staffing and persons served and to evaluator access [19]. The conditions followed a preliminary report of September 2025 in which the state's independent evaluator said it "cannot verify" the emergency-room and jail-diversion figures the Department had reported [14]. <!-- b: B0028 -->

- **The funding surge did not continue.** The FY2027 General Fund appropriation of $240.9 million is slightly below FY2026's $244.5 million [18] [20]. For FY2026 the Department had been considering, in December 2024, a request for a 22.9 percent increase in its total state appropriation (28.8 percent in General Fund terms) [21]; the enacted General Fund increase was about 2 percent [20] [22]. Alabama's state mental health agency spent $133 per resident in FY2024 against a national average of $179 [23]. <!-- b: B0029 -->

- **The biggest structural change under way is payment reform, not new buildings.** In June 2024 Alabama was named one of ten states added to the federal CCBHC Medicaid demonstration [24] [25]. Four clinics have moved to the model — two certified by the state in July 2024 and two approved by SAMHSA in October 2025 and April 2026 [26] [27] — and ADMH's CCBHC directory lists a fifth, which the Department's May 2026 release does not name [28]; six more providers received grants in August 2026 to pursue certification [29], and the Department's stated goal is a statewide transition [24] [27]. The first two clinics served 30,275 people in FY2025 [16]. <!-- b: B0030 -->

- **Beds remain far below the planning benchmark.** Alabama had 15.1 involuntary (civil-commitment) beds per 100,000 people in January 2026 — 771 beds, up from 698 beds (13.7 per 100,000) in August 2025 — against the 30 per 100,000 that the Treatment Advocacy Center calls the minimum and the Department uses as its planning benchmark [30] [31]. The Department prices closing the 779-bed gap to that benchmark at $226.4 million [30]; the House General Fund budget chair has put the cost of replacing 787 beds at about $590 million in construction alone [32] [33]. The two are separate estimates, not reconciled in the sources. The forensic waitlist — people waiting in county jails for psychiatric evaluation or treatment — stood at 291 in July 2026, under a federal consent decree [32] [11]. <!-- b: B0031 -->

- **Workforce is a binding constraint on every other domain.** 2.9 million Alabamians live in federally designated mental-health shortage areas; the state meets 28 percent of the estimated need for psychiatric care in those areas, as of December 2025 [34]. Mental Health America ranks Alabama 51st of 51 for workforce availability, at one provider per 740 residents [35]. <!-- b: B0032 -->

- **Coverage is the equity issue.** Alabama has not expanded Medicaid [36]. The coverage gap is put at up to 155,000 people by Alabama Arise [36] [37] (KFF's July 2026 estimate is 86,000 uninsured adults in the gap itself and 147,000 who would become eligible if the state expanded [38]), and roughly 95,000 people left the ACA marketplace in 2026 after enhanced subsidies expired [36] [39]. Among adults with mental illness, 14.1 percent are uninsured against 9.2 percent nationally [35]. <!-- b: B0033 -->

- **Children's access to care is where Alabama ranks worst.** On Mental Health America's youth measures Alabama ranks 50th of 51; nearly two-thirds (66.4 percent) of Alabama youth with major depression receive no treatment, the worst rate in the country [35]. The state's own plan lists child and family services second among its five priorities [40], and the pediatric crisis unit in Huntsville — the state's first [41] — performed 520 evaluations in FY2025 [16]. <!-- b: B0034 -->

- **The data to manage the system is improving but still thin.** The 2025 federal reporting shows a public-system penetration rate of 18.3 per 1,000 residents against 22.9 nationally (18.3 and 23.5 in 2024) [42] [43]. The state's evaluation commission, created in 2019, published a preliminary evaluation of the crisis centers in September 2025 [44] [14], and Alabama's health information exchange listed the Department's connection as a work in progress as of December 2025 [45]. <!-- b: B0035 -->

## Using the report for advocacy

- **Cite the figure and its owner.** Each section names the responsible body. *The Department's own annual report shows 637 people served in its mental-illness facilities in FY2025, and the Department told the Joint Mental Health Task Force in August 2026 that its forensic waitlist stood at 291 in July* carries more weight than a general claim of shortage [16] [32]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0037 -->

- **Match the ask to the domain.** A county can act on jail screening (Section 10) [46] and transit (Section 11) [47]; the Legislature acts on appropriations (Domain 2) [18] and licensure compacts (Domain 4) [48]; the Department acts on certification and crisis-system design (Domains 1 and 3) [49] [50]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0038 -->

- **Use the state's own plan.** Every domain section lists the goals of the ADMH Strategic Plan 2025–2028 it touches [40] (the goal numbering is Little Orange Fish's own, from its alignment analysis of the full plan). Framing a request as support for a goal the state has already adopted is both accurate and effective. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0039 -->

- **Acknowledge gains.** Crisis centers, 988, CCBHC, the pediatric crisis unit and the Taylor Hardin expansion are real [12] [9] [27] [41] [32]. Advocacy that credits them and then shows the next gap is harder to dismiss. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0040 -->

- **Keep to what is verified.** Appendix D lists what this report could not confirm. Do not fill those gaps with estimates in public materials. The "Gaps and opportunities" bullets are this report's reading of the evidence, not positions. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0041 -->

- **Plan across the continuum.** A program that adds crisis capacity without a step-down or follow-up partner adds readmissions. The continuum-lens paragraphs in Domains 2 and 4 to 8 show which neighbors a program needs [3]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0044 -->

- **Measure against the baseline.** Appendix B gives Alabama's standing on 17 national indicators and the federal Uniform Reporting System measures. Later editions will use the same table. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0045 -->

# Part II. The Eight Domains

<!-- section: d1 -->
## Domain 1. Governance, Policy and Legal Foundations

<!-- section: d1-scope -->
### Scope

Governance is the set of laws, institutions and decision rights that determine who is responsible for mental health in Alabama, how they are held to account, and what rights people with mental illness can assert. It includes the state mental health authority and its board, the Legislature's budget and policy committees, the Governor, the courts that enforce constitutional standards of care, and the interagency bodies that coordinate across health, education, justice and human services. A strong governance frame gives the rest of the system direction, legitimacy and accountability; a weak one leaves gaps that courts eventually fill. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0055 -->

<!-- section: d1-alabama-s-structures -->
### Alabama's structures

**The Alabama Department of Mental Health (ADMH)** is the state mental health authority, created by statute in 1965 and governed by Title 22, Chapter 50 of the Code of Alabama, which authorizes it "to act in any prudent way to provide mental health services" to Alabamians [51]. The Governor appoints the Commissioner, who serves at the Governor's pleasure [51]. Kimberly G. Boswell has been Commissioner since December 2020 [50]. <!-- b: B0057 -->

The Department is organized into three divisions. The **Division of Mental Health and Substance Use Services** (Associate Commissioner Nicole Walden) holds the offices of Certification; Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics; Child and Family Services; Deaf Services; Facility Operations; Forensic Mental Health Services; Forensic Outpatient Services; Mental Illness Community Programs; Peer Programs; Quality Improvement and Risk Management; and Substance Use Services [16] [52]. The **Division of Developmental Disabilities** (Associate Commissioner Camille Cumuze) serves people with intellectual and developmental disabilities [53]. The **Division of Administration** (Associate Commissioner Brooke Hibbard) runs finance, certification administration, policy and planning, information systems and facilities [16] [54]. Both the Developmental Disabilities and Administration associate commissioner positions changed hands in 2025; the board's November 2025 minutes record the retirements and introduce the successors [55] [56]. <!-- b: B0058 -->

The 2025 edition of this report described separate associate commissioners for "mental illness" and "substance abuse". That structure no longer exists; mental health and substance use are one division [16] [52]. <!-- b: B0059 -->

**The ADMH Board of Trustees** has sixteen members. The Governor chairs it; the Speaker of the House, the Lieutenant Governor and the Commissioner (as secretary) sit ex officio, with seven congressional-district and five at-large appointees. The board meets quarterly and publishes its minutes [57] [55] [26]. <!-- b: B0060 -->

**The ADMH Strategic Plan 2025–2028** sets five priorities [40]. (The goal numbering used in this report — A1 to E3 — follows Little Orange Fish's alignment analysis of the full plan document; the Department's strategy page shows the five priority headings [40].) <!-- b: B0061 -->

1. **System of Care Transformation** — "transform the system of care to ensure services are accessible, integrated, and responsive." [40] <!-- b: B0062 -->

2. **Child and Family Services.** [40] <!-- b: B0063 -->

3. **Workforce Health and Wellness** — recruit and retain a skilled workforce. [40] <!-- b: B0064 -->

4. **Resources** — seek and secure sustainable funding. [40] <!-- b: B0065 -->

5. **Awareness and Knowledge** — empower Alabamians with clear, relevant information. [40] <!-- b: B0066 -->

The numeric targets associated with the plan in public discussion — civil psychiatric beds from 13.4 to 30 per 100,000, mobile crisis teams from 14 to 23, crisis centers from 6 to 11, and crisis coverage from 24 counties to all 67 — come from the Commissioner's February 2025 budget presentation, as reported by Alabama Daily News, rather than the strategy document itself, and should be cited that way [33]. (The Department's annual report gives 13.7 beds per 100,000 for the start of FY2025, and its August 2025 presentation gives the same figure; its FY2027 budget presentation, counting beds as of 13 January 2026, gives 15.1 [16] [31] [30].) The 2025 edition of this report stated that ADMH had no single strategic plan or annual report; both exist, and the FY2025 annual report (posted April 2026) is this report's most-used source [16] [40]. <!-- b: B0067 -->

**The Legislature** enacts the General Fund and Education Trust Fund appropriations that are among the Department's largest state revenues (Domain 2) [16] [20] [58], and in the 2026 session attached conditions to them: as reported on final passage, the FY2027 General Fund budget withholds the last two quarterly installments of the $42 million for crisis diversion and rural crisis-care centers until the Department reports the status of the programs to the General Fund budget chairs, the Finance Director and the Legislative Fiscal Officer [17] [18]; the Senate committee's version of the bill had required reports of each crisis center's operating expenses, employees and persons served, and access for the state evaluator [19]. The House General Fund budget chair, Rep. Rex Reynolds, has been a prominent legislative voice on crisis-system funding [33]. Legislation in the 2025 session touching mental health included SB101, raising the age of independent consent for medical and mental-health treatment from 14 to 16 (Act 2025-455, effective 1 October 2025) [59] [60]; SB236 (Act 2025-366), which requires the executive committee of a regional mental health authority board with more than sixteen directors to include a sheriff (or the sheriff's representative) and a probate judge where those officials serve on the board [61] [62]; SB200 (Act 2025-183), which renamed drug courts "accountability courts" and extended their eligibility to defendants with mental illness and to veterans [63] [62]; SB40, the Houston Hunter Act, creating voluntary firearm-hold agreements as a suicide-prevention measure [64]; and SB70, creating a Veterans Resource Center [22]. No comparable mental-health statute from the 2026 session was identified in the sources read for this report (as of October 2026). <!-- b: B0068 -->

**Independent evaluation** now extends to the crisis system. The Alabama Commission on the Evaluation of Services (ACES), created by Act 2019-517 (Code of Alabama §29-2A-1) to advise the Legislature and the Governor [44], evaluated the state's suicide-prevention programs, including the Department's, in 2020 [65] and published a preliminary evaluation of the crisis centers in September 2025 [14]; the FY2027 appropriation conditions followed [19]. <!-- b: B0069 -->

<!-- section: d1-legal-foundations -->
### Legal foundations

Three bodies of law frame the system. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0071 -->

**Civil commitment** (Code of Alabama §22-52) allows any person to petition the probate court; the standard is clear and convincing evidence of mental illness and a real and present threat of substantial harm, and the court must order the least restrictive alternative necessary and available [66]. Initial orders run up to 150 days and renewals up to one year [66]. Outpatient commitment is available where a designated facility consents [66]. No annual count of commitments was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0072 -->

**Constitutional standards of care** were set in Alabama. _Wyatt v. Stickney_ (M.D. Ala., 1971) established a right to adequate treatment for people confined in state psychiatric institutions and kept the Department under federal court supervision until 2003 [67] [68]. Its successors are active today. In _Hunter v. Beshear_ (settled 2018; now captioned _Hunter v. Boswell_), a consent decree governs the time people may wait in jail for competency evaluation and restoration; the decree's inpatient-restoration term, extended in March 2025, runs to 31 January 2027, with the Alabama Disabilities Advocacy Program, an associational plaintiff, monitoring compliance [11]. In _Braggs v. Commissioner, Alabama Department of Corrections_ (the Eleventh Circuit's caption; the case is now styled _Braggs v. Lovelace_), a 2017 ruling found that mental health care in state prisons violated the Eighth Amendment; on 24 June 2026 the Eleventh Circuit affirmed that finding and the system-wide injunction but reversed the specific correctional-staffing mandate, some suicide-resistant cell requirements and the application of relief to Tutwiler, and returned those questions to the district court [69] [70] [71]. A separate U.S. Department of Justice suit against the Department of Corrections is set for trial in November 2026 [72]. <!-- b: B0073 -->

**Federal program law** — the Mental Health Block Grant, Medicaid's state plan and waivers, and since 2024 the Section 223 CCBHC demonstration — carries its own planning, reporting and consumer-participation requirements, including the federally required state mental health planning council [73] [24] [74] [26]. <!-- b: B0074 -->

### Gaps and opportunities

- **Accountability now runs through appropriations.** The FY2027 conditions on crisis-center funds show the Legislature using the budget as its oversight tool [17] [19]. The opportunity is to agree, in advance, on a small set of published measures (Domain 6) so that conditions are met with data rather than negotiated after the fact. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0078 -->

- **Coordination across sectors is ad hoc.** No standing statewide body dedicated to mental health that joins the Department, the courts, the Department of Corrections, Pardons and Paroles, Human Resources, Youth Services and Education was identified for this report; the Reentry Task Force created by SJR80 (2025) is the nearest thing on the justice side [77], and for children state law provides two bodies with wider remits, the State Team for multiple-needs children [78] and the Children's Policy Council [79]. According to Little Orange Fish's alignment analysis of the full plan, the ADMH plan's child-and-family priority includes identifying shared objectives with child-serving agencies (goal B4 in this report's numbering), which implies such a forum (Section 9). <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0079 -->

- **Court orders still substitute for policy.** More than twenty years after _Wyatt_ ended, Alabama operates its forensic and prison mental-health systems under two federal decrees [68] [11] [69]. Meeting the _Hunter_ timelines by January 2027 is a governance deadline as much as a clinical one (Section 10) [11]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0080 -->

- **Coverage policy is unresolved.** The Legislature has not expanded Medicaid [36], and no alternative coverage program was found to have been enacted as of October 2026; the 2025 federal budget law ended, from January 2026, the temporary enhanced federal match offered to states that newly expand and limits state provider taxes (Section 12) [80] [81]. Coverage policy affects more sections of this report than any other single decision. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0081 -->

- **Regulatory modernization continues.** Alabama belongs to seven interstate licensure compacts (Domain 4) [82] [48] [83] [84] [85] [86] [87] and permits audio-only Medicaid telehealth at parity (Domain 5) [88]; keeping rules current with practice remains a standing task. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0082 -->

<!-- section: d2 -->
## Domain 2. Financing and Resource Allocation

<!-- section: d2-scope -->
### Scope

Financing covers where the money for mental health care comes from — state appropriations, Medicaid and other federal funds, commercial insurance, local and philanthropic sources — and how it is allocated across hospitals, community programs, crisis services and the workforce. The question for planners is not only how much Alabama spends, but which payer pays for which service, because that determines which services can grow. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0090 -->

<!-- section: d2-the-department-s-budget -->
### The Department's budget

ADMH's total operating revenue in FY2025 was **$1.43 billion**. The state's share was 40.9 percent: General Fund $239.8 million (16.8 percent), the Special Mental Health Fund $236.4 million (16.5 percent), the Education Trust Fund $79.1 million (5.5 percent), and other state sources $30.7 million, of which the Opioid Treatment Fund was $12.5 million. The remaining 59.1 percent was federal, local and miscellaneous, dominated by Medicaid: $568.0 million for community developmental-disability services, $125.9 million for community mental-illness services, $14.2 million for facilities and $9.5 million for community substance-use services. Federal grants added $64.2 million and federal block grants $33.8 million [16]. <!-- b: B0092 -->

Expenditures were $1.45 billion, of which 87.4 percent went to community services — $772.1 million developmental disabilities, $400.9 million mental illness, $89.9 million substance use — 8.5 percent (about $123 million) to the state hospitals (Bryce $55.9 million, Taylor Hardin $34.7 million, Mary Starke Harper $30.7 million, East Alabama adolescent unit $1.5 million), 2.6 percent to administration and the balance to special services [16]. <!-- b: B0093 -->

Two points follow. First, more than half of ADMH's budget is developmental-disability services funded largely by Medicaid; the mental-illness share of community spending is about $401 million [16]. Second, the General Fund — the line the Legislature debates each spring — is one-sixth of the whole [16], but it is the part that pays for what Medicaid does not: crisis centers, state hospitals for the uninsured, and services for adults without coverage. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0094 -->

### What the Legislature did, FY2026 and FY2027

<!-- section: d3 -->
## Domain 3. The Service-Delivery Continuum

<!-- section: d3-scope -->
### Scope

This domain describes what services exist in Alabama, who provides them, and how a person moves between them: from promotion and prevention, through outpatient treatment, crisis response, inpatient and forensic care, to the supports that keep recovery going. The organizing question, borrowed from the National Academies' continuum and from the state mental health directors' _Beyond Beds_ analysis, is whether each level exists at enough scale and whether the hand-offs between levels work [2] [3]. <!-- b: B0126 -->

<!-- section: d3-who-was-served -->
### Who was served

In FY2025 the public system served about 232,000 people: 141,631 adults and 31,847 adolescents for mental illness, 48,860 adults and 4,506 adolescents for substance use (figures the Department marks as estimated for FY2025), and 4,817 adults and 340 adolescents for developmental disabilities. Community mental health center programs served 96,062 people, and the two then-certified CCBHCs served 30,275 [16]. The federal Uniform Reporting System counts 94,410 Alabamians served by the state mental health authority in its 2025 reporting year (October 2024 to September 2025), up from 93,506 in 2024, a penetration rate of 18.31 per 1,000 residents in both years against 22.94 nationally (23.46 in 2024); 618 were served in state hospitals during the year (645 in 2024) [42] [43]. <!-- b: B0128 -->

<!-- section: d3-the-community-base-19-centers-becoming -->
### The community base: 19 centers becoming CCBHCs

Alabama's community mental health system rests on **19 community mental health centers (CMHCs)** [31], each governed by a regional "310" board [49] and together covering all 67 counties: ADMH's emergency-contact page lists a community provider for every county [107]. (ADMH's FY2025 report counts 23 CMHC programs [16], reflecting providers that operate more than one program; 19 is the number of organizations.) They are the entry point for outpatient therapy, psychiatric medication management, case management, Assertive Community Treatment, day programs, residential care, supported housing and supported employment for adults with serious mental illness and children with serious emotional disturbance. The March 2026 contract service delivery manual lists the residential tiers ADMH will fund — among them supported housing, adult residential care homes, three-bed residential, semi-independent apartments, crisis residential units, transitional-age residential and foster care [108]. <!-- b: B0130 -->

The centers are in the middle of a structural change. In June 2024 Alabama was named one of ten states added to the federal **Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic (CCBHC)** Medicaid demonstration [24] [25]. A CCBHC must provide nine services, including 24/7 crisis response, outpatient mental-health and substance-use treatment, primary-care screening, targeted case management, psychiatric rehabilitation, peer and family support and services for veterans, and is paid a daily prospective rate rather than fee-for-service [10]. WellStone (Huntsville) and AltaPointe (Mobile) were certified in July 2024 [26]; Mountain Lakes Behavioral Healthcare (Jackson and Marshall counties) received federal approval in October 2025 and Highland Health Systems (Calhoun and Cleburne) in April 2026 [27]. ADMH's CCBHC service directory also lists a fifth clinic, South Central Alabama Mental Health, which the May 2026 release does not name [28] [27]. Six more providers — Integrea, Northwest Alabama Mental Health Center, Carastar, CarePath, Central Alabama Wellness and JBS — received rural-health grants in August 2026 to pursue certification [29]. ADMH's stated goal is a statewide transition [24] [27]. Section 12 describes the payment model. <!-- b: B0131 -->

Three **first-episode psychosis** (coordinated specialty care) programs operate under the NOVA name — at JBS Mental Health Authority in Birmingham, AltaPointe in Mobile and WellStone in Huntsville [109]. Statewide in the 2025 reporting year, 897 people were in Assertive Community Treatment, 234 in supported employment and 259 in supported housing (779, 205 and 240 in 2024) [42] [43]. <!-- b: B0132 -->

<!-- section: d3-crisis-response-someone-to-contact -->
### Crisis response: someone to contact, someone to respond, a safe place for help

Alabama built its crisis system almost from nothing after 2020. It now has the three elements of the national model [110] [13], and in 2025 its crisis centers came under independent evaluation by the Alabama Commission on the Evaluation of Services [14]. <!-- b: B0134 -->

**Someone to contact.** Alabama answers 988 through four in-state call centers operated by AltaPointe (Mobile), JBS and the Crisis Center (Birmingham), SpectraCare (Dothan) and WellStone (Huntsville) [9]. The centers handled 40,053 calls in FY2025, up from 34,027 in FY2024; texts and chats were still routed to a national center. The in-state answer rate — calls answered by an Alabama counselor rather than routed to a national backup — rose from 82.5 percent in FY2024 to 87.4 percent in FY2025 and reached 90.9 percent in May 2025; average speed to answer was 22 seconds by September 2025 [16]. Between the July 2022 launch and ADMH's release of 16 July 2025, crisis counselors received 132,713 contacts, counting calls, texts and chats [15]. The FY2027 General Fund line for 988 call centers is zero, after $500,000 in FY2026 [18]; opioid-settlement appropriations carried $3.5 million for the 988 crisis line in 2025 and $4 million in the 2026 supplemental appropriation the Legislature passed in April (HB487) [91] [92]. <!-- b: B0135 -->

**Someone to respond.** ADMH funds **14 mobile crisis teams at 11 CMHCs** [13]; in February 2025 the Department's five-year plan was to grow from 14 teams to 23, and 24 counties were then covered by mobile teams and crisis centers taken together [33]. Five child-and-adolescent teams at three centers (AltaPointe, JBS, WellStone) [111] served 936 young people in FY2025, the first year that figure was reported [16]; the General Fund has carried a $5 million child-and-adolescent mobile-crisis pilot line since at least FY2025, unchanged in FY2027 [20] [18]. Mobile crisis became a Medicaid-billable service in October 2023 [75]. A co-responder program pairing officers with clinicians was running in Huntsville by early 2022 [112], and Mobile police announced a one-precinct pilot with AltaPointe in May 2022 [113] (Section 10). <!-- b: B0136 -->

**A safe place for help.** Six **crisis diversion centers** (the state budget's term; ADMH's own pages call them crisis centers [20] [12]) operate — AltaPointe (Mobile), Carastar (Montgomery), JBS (Birmingham), Indian Rivers' Hope Pointe (Tuscaloosa), WellStone (Huntsville) and SpectraCare (Dothan, operational from 17 February 2025) [12] [114] — plus WellStone's 24-bed **pediatric crisis unit** (ages 12–18) in Huntsville, the state's first, opened in late September 2024 [41] [115]. The centers accept walk-ins and law-enforcement drop-offs around the clock [12] and provide crisis stabilization, clinical evaluation and psychiatric care, with temporary and extended observation beds [12] [31]; the opioid council's 2025 report describes them as "detox-capable" [76]. They performed 9,929 evaluations in FY2025 and more than 1,000 in August 2025, a new monthly high; the pediatric unit performed 520 [16]. ADMH's five-year plan of February 2025 called for eleven centers [33]; the FY2026 and FY2027 General Fund budgets fund the "Existing Six Crisis Diversion Centers" and carry no line for a seventh [20] [18]. The 2025 edition of this report gave the centers' catchment as 20 of 67 counties; ADMH's crisis-centers page now lists 60 counties as served by one of the six centers and leaves out seven (Butler, Coffee, Colbert, Covington, Crenshaw, Franklin, Lauderdale) [12] [13] — a measure of responsibility rather than travel distance (the 24 counties reported in February 2025 counted counties then covered by mobile teams and crisis centers together [33]). ADMH's own pages disagree on the detail: that page lists five counties for the Dothan center, while the Department's February 2025 release says it serves 20 [12] [114]. <!-- b: B0137 -->

**What the evaluator found.** The Alabama Commission on the Evaluation of Services said in a preliminary report of September 2025 that the centers then operating had logged 16,414 interactions from 2021 to the end of 2024 and that the state had dedicated $175 million to the six centers since FY2021; that the FY2024 average was $2,193 per bed-day and 27 percent utilization; that capital costs ranged from $3.3 million to $12.6 million per center; and that it "cannot verify" the emergency-room and jail-diversion figures the Department had reported, because centers counted them inconsistently and one never reported a jail diversion. The Department's response, printed with the evaluation, questions how utilization and average daily bed costs were calculated and says "most centers function at capacity most days" [14]; at a public meeting that September the Commissioner and her staff pushed back on the analysis, saying the Department cannot turn over protected health information [19]. The FY2027 budget then attached conditions to the crisis money: the Senate committee's version made the third- and fourth-quarter payments for the six centers depend on reporting of each center's operating expenses, employees and persons served, and on evaluator access [19]; the enacted budget, as reported, withholds the last two quarterly installments of the $42 million for crisis centers and rural crisis care until the Department "reports the status of the programs" to the budget chairs, the Finance Director and the Legislative Fiscal Officer [17]. Provider-level examples remain available — SpectraCare reported 485 emergency-room diversions and 43 law-enforcement drop-offs in the part of FY2025 after it opened [16] — but the 2025 edition's statewide diversion totals should no longer be cited. <!-- b: B0138 -->

<!-- section: d3-inpatient-and-forensic-care -->
### Inpatient and forensic care

Alabama operates three state psychiatric facilities: **Bryce Hospital** (Tuscaloosa; 337 people served in FY2025), **Taylor Hardin Secure Medical Facility** (forensic; 189) and the **Mary Starke Harper Geriatric Psychiatry Center** (94), plus an adolescent unit at East Alabama Medical Center (17), for a combined 637 individuals served in FY2025 as reported by ADMH [16]; the federal tables count 618 people served in state hospitals during the 2025 reporting year and 645 in 2024 [42] [43]. <!-- b: B0140 -->

Capacity is the system's most-cited gap. ADMH's FY2027 budget presentation, with data as of 13 January 2026, counts **771 involuntary (civil-commitment) beds** — 285 at Bryce and Harper, 234 contracted in community hospitals and 252 crisis residential beds at certified providers, which ADMH lists among its involuntary beds — or **15.1 per 100,000 residents against the 30 per 100,000** (1,550 beds) that the Treatment Advocacy Center calls the minimum and the Department uses as its planning benchmark; the presentation prices closing the 779-bed gap, as a five-year goal, at $226.4 million [30]. The earlier count was 698 beds (13.7 per 100,000), both at the start of FY2025 and in August 2025 — 285 at Bryce and Harper, 225 contracted and 188 crisis residential — plus 180 voluntary observation beds at crisis centers; the Department's stated intention then was to add 57 beds over the following year, to 755 (14.8 per 100,000), funded from the Opioid Settlement Fund and legislative appropriation [16] [31]. In August 2026 the House General Fund budget chair, Rep. Rex Reynolds, put the cost of replacing 787 beds at about $590 million in construction alone; the 2010 cuts had closed 725 [32] [33]. The $226.4 million and the $590 million are two separate estimates, not reconciled in the sources. <!-- b: B0141 -->

<!-- figure: fig-beds -->

On the forensic side, Taylor Hardin has expanded from 140 to 225 beds, with about 200 filled by August 2026 [32] [115]. The forensic waitlist — people waiting in county jails for psychiatric evaluation or treatment, the population covered by the _Hunter_ consent decree — fell from 327 in January 2026 to 291 in July 2026, against a goal of 100 by year-end [32]; the average wait was "well over a year" in November 2025 [116]. A jail-based restoration program had enrolled 31 people and completed 23 by September 2025 [16]; the Department has also begun a telehealth restoration program in county jails, and forensic navigators now work the list [32]. Section 10 treats this in full. <!-- b: B0142 -->

Community hospitals and emergency departments remain the default for many people in crisis — ADMH names reducing "the burden on EDs/Hospitals" among the goals of crisis care [13] — but emergency-department boarding of psychiatric patients is not measured statewide: no such measure was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026. Children's of Alabama said in January 2025 that it would add eleven beds to its inpatient behavioral-health unit that month [117]; UAB's child and adolescent psychiatry division, which describes a strong on-site affiliation with the hospital, lists 50 inpatient beds (12 child, 38 adolescent) on an undated page [118]. <!-- b: B0143 -->

<!-- section: d3-promotion-prevention-and-early -->
### Promotion, prevention and early intervention

- **School-based mental health.** ADMH's collaboration with the State Department of Education brings CMHC services into **122 school systems** through all 19 centers [119], funded at $5.5 million in the FY2027 ETF [58]; separately, local education agencies that apply receive roughly $40,000 each for a Mental Health Services Coordinator under an ALSDE program [120]. The sources disagree on that program's FY2026 funding: ALSDE's memo says the FY2026 ETF appropriated $6.36 million [120], while the Legislature's FY2027 ETF sheet shows the line at $4.69 million budgeted for FY2026 and $9.25 million in the FY2027 ETF [58]. Since 2022 schools must obtain written parental opt-in consent for mental-health services, at first for students under 14 [60] [121]; since October 2025 students under 16 cannot consent to ongoing counseling on their own [59] [60]. <!-- b: B0145 -->

- **Suicide prevention.** ADMH employs the state's suicide prevention coordinator, the Alabama Suicide Prevention and Resources Coalition is the state coalition, and the state plan runs 2021–2026 [122]; 834 Alabamians died by suicide in 2024, a rate of 16.2 per 100,000 [123]. The Houston Hunter Act (2025) adds voluntary firearm-hold agreements as a lethal-means tool [64]. <!-- b: B0146 -->

- **Substance-use prevention and treatment.** The Opioid Overdose and Addiction Council's 2025 report counts at least 1,151 overdose deaths in 2024, a floor drawn from emergency-department cases, and an estimated 1,052 in 2025, a provisional figure [76]; ADMH distributed 48,179 boxes of naloxone between 1 November 2024 and 31 October 2025 (40,733 kits in fiscal year 2025) [76] and certifies 104 detoxification beds statewide, including a 16-bed unit in Huntsville [16]. <!-- b: B0147 -->

- **Primary care.** Alabama's federally qualified health centers are a primary-care platform for integrated behavioral health: the state's 17 federally funded health centers served 316,993 patients in 2025, 15,696 of them for mental health [124]. Alabama Medicaid pays for live-video and audio-only telehealth at parity, which supports collaborative-care models [88]. No statewide count of integrated-care sites was found as of October 2026. Alabama Medicaid is a fee-for-service program with no comprehensive managed-care plans [125] [49], with care coordination through the Alabama Coordinated Health Network [126], so behavioral-health benefits are set by the state plan and ADMH certification rather than by health plans [49] (Section 12). <!-- b: B0148 -->

- **Peer support.** ADMH's Office of Peer Programs trains and certifies peer specialists [127]; a current statewide count was not found on its page or in the Department's other published sources read as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0149 -->

<!-- section: d3-recovery-supports -->
### Recovery supports

Supported employment reached 234 people and supported housing 259 in the 2025 reporting year (205 and 240 in 2024), small numbers against 94,410 served [42] [43]. The Individual Placement and Support program, run with the Department of Rehabilitation Services, expanded in February 2025 from three sites to five (Central Alabama Wellness, AltaPointe, Carastar, Indian Rivers, JBS) under a five-year federal grant [128]. In 2025, 91.4 percent of people served (all ages) lived in a private residence and 2.7 percent were homeless or in a shelter at the reporting point (92.7 and 1.9 percent in 2024); among adults the 2025 figures were 87.6 and 4.1 percent. Of adults served, 21.0 percent were employed against 28.8 percent nationally (21.7 and 28.9 percent in 2024) [42] [43]. Section 11 covers housing and employment as systems. <!-- b: B0151 -->

<!-- section: d3-continuity-of-care -->
### Continuity of care

Thirty-day readmission to state hospitals was 1.5 percent in the 2025 reporting year (9.0 percent nationally) and 180-day readmission also 1.5 percent (19.8 percent nationally), down from 6.8 and 9.5 percent in 2024; the rates rest on very small numbers — one readmission among 68 civil discharges in 2025, five among 74 in 2024 [42] [43]. The 2025 edition's figure of 3.4 percent reflected an earlier year. No statewide seven-day follow-up measure was found in the Department's published reports or the federal tables as of October 2026; the CCBHC billing manual allows follow-up interventions for up to 72 hours after a mobile-crisis response [105]. <!-- b: B0153 -->

### Gaps and opportunities

- **Measure the crisis system the way the evaluator asked.** Reporting to the Legislature on the crisis programs is now a condition of the last two quarterly payments [17]; the Senate committee's version specified operating costs, staffing and persons served, plus evaluator access [19]. The evaluator found no common way of counting diversion among the centers [14], and no uniform definitions of follow-up or readmission were found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026. Extending common definitions to mobile crisis and 988 would give Alabama a crisis dashboard for the first time. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0157 -->

- **Fill the middle of the continuum.** Beds and crisis centers have grown; step-down residential, intensive outpatient and supported housing have not. The 2025 figures of 259 people in supported housing and 234 in supported employment measure the gap [42]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0158 -->

- **Reach the counties without teams.** In February 2025 mobile crisis teams and crisis centers together covered 24 counties [33]; the five rural crisis-care programs, first funded in FY2021 [111] and carried at $6 million a year in the General Fund [18], and the $5 million child-and-adolescent pilot, in the budget since at least FY2025 [20] [18], are the current increments toward the 23-team goal [33]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0159 -->

<!-- section: d4 -->
## Domain 4. Workforce Development and Well-being

<!-- section: d4-scope -->
### Scope

The workforce domain covers the people who deliver mental health care — psychiatrists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, psychologists, licensed counselors and social workers, nurses, case managers, direct-support staff, peer specialists — and the pipeline, licensure, incentives and working conditions that determine how many there are and where they practice. It also covers the wider network of first responders, educators and faith leaders trained to recognize and respond to mental health needs. Every other domain depends on this one. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0169 -->

<!-- section: d4-alabama-s-shortage-measured -->
### Alabama's shortage, measured

Alabama has **67 federally designated mental health professional shortage areas** covering **2,934,202 people** — more than half the state's population of 5.19 million [34] [133]. Federal data as of December 2025 show Alabama meeting **28.4 percent** of the need for psychiatric care in those areas, with 128 additional practitioners required to lift the designations [34]. Mental Health America's 2025 ranking places Alabama **51st of 51 for mental health workforce availability**, at one provider for every 740 residents against a national ratio of one to 320 [35]. In this report's reading, the shortage falls hardest on the 42 percent of the population that lives in rural areas [134]. <!-- b: B0171 -->

Inside the public system, ADMH reported in 2024 that its mental health provider network had 752 vacancies at the end of FY2023 against a need of 4,678 full-time positions — about 3,926 filled, or about 84 percent staffed [135]. No later statewide staffing or vacancy figure was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026; its 2025 and 2026 budget presentations did not update it, and this report does not estimate one (Appendix D). The Commissioner told the board in August 2024 that only about half of the forensic hospital's technician and nursing positions were filled [116] [136]. <!-- b: B0172 -->

<!-- section: d4-how-alabama-develops-its-workforce -->
### How Alabama develops its workforce

**Licensure compacts.** Alabama has joined most of the interstate compacts that let licensed clinicians practice across state lines, in person or by telehealth: the Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact (PSYPACT, effective June 2021) [48], the Counseling Compact (member; ten states were issuing privileges by 1 October 2026, Alabama not yet among them) [82], the Nurse Licensure Compact (January 2020) [83], the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact [84], the EMS Compact (2017) [85] and the Occupational Therapy Compact (member, not yet issuing privileges in Alabama) [86]. Alabama joined the Social Work Licensure Compact in 2024 [87]; that compact had 35 member states by July 2026 but had not yet begun issuing multistate licenses [137]. <!-- b: B0174 -->

**Training pipeline.** The University of Alabama's College of Community Health Sciences announced a psychiatry residency with its first class to start in June 2026 and six residents a year [138], and HB487 (2026) directs $2 million of opioid-settlement funds to a UAB psychiatry residency [92] [93]; UAB's child and adolescent psychiatry division trains eight fellows [118]. Alabama's universities — UAB, the University of Alabama, Auburn, the University of South Alabama, Alabama A&M, Alabama State and others — supply counseling, social work, psychology and nursing graduates; no statewide behavioral-health graduate-output series was found as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0175 -->

**Incentives.** Federal National Health Service Corps loan repayment is available at approved shortage-area sites [139]. In FY2024 the Legislature funded $15 million for mental-illness providers and $2.86 million for substance-use providers as workforce investments through higher provider rates, and the rate increases it funded for developmental-disability providers lifted the average direct-support-professional wage from $9.91 an hour in FY2022 to $15.17 by March 2024; ADMH added shift differentials and attendance bonuses for state-hospital staff [135] [140]. The FY2026 budget carried provider rate-increase lines for intellectual-disability programs, raised by $2.5 million, and for substance-use programs, held at the FY2025 level [20]. No direct-support wage later than March 2024 was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0176 -->

**Peer and community workforce.** ADMH's Office of Peer Programs trains and certifies peer support specialists [127]; the CCBHC model requires peer and family support as one of its nine services, which will expand demand for certified peers as centers convert [10]. The Agency for Substance Abuse Prevention, with ADMH, has certified more than 400 faith leaders as Faith-Based Support Specialists since 2022 [141]. The Alabama Peace Officers' Standards and Training Commission has listed a two-day Crisis Intervention Team class for 16 continuing-education units but mandates no mental-health hours in officers' continuing education [142] [143]; NAMI Alabama's CIT Division coordinates the regional CIT programs and its pages give no statewide count of trained officers [144] [145]; ADMH's July 2024 budget presentation reported "1375 plus CIT trained officers" [135], and no current or agency-level count was found as of October 2026 (Section 10). <!-- b: B0177 -->

**Strategic plan.** Workforce Health and Wellness is the third of ADMH's five priorities [40]; according to Little Orange Fish's alignment analysis of the full plan, its goals are to support staff well-being, build trauma-related skills, train supervisors and widen the applicant pool through alternative qualifications. <!-- b: B0178 -->

<!-- section: d4-well-being-and-retention -->
### Well-being and retention

The Department's plan, as read in Little Orange Fish's alignment analysis, names well-being first among its workforce goals, a recognition that turnover, not only recruitment, drains capacity. Public evidence is thin: no statewide turnover rate, burnout survey or exit data was found for the mental health provider network or the state hospitals in the Department's published reports as of October 2026; its FY2026 budget presentation says only that Alabama was "one of the states with the largest decrease in staff turnover" in 2022–2023 National Core Indicators data [140]. The Legislature's 2025 resolution (HJR273) commissioned a study of PTSD among first responders [22]. What can be said is that pay has moved — the direct-support wage rose by half between 2022 and 2024 [135] — and that the forensic system's staffing shortfall is the most visible well-being problem in the system, because it determines how long people wait in jail. <!-- b: B0180 -->

<!-- section: d4-continuum-lens -->
### Continuum lens

Workforce shortages fall unevenly along the continuum. Crisis centers and 988 have been staffed because they were funded first [135]; outpatient and children's services carry the longest waits because they depend on licensed clinicians in shortage areas [34]; the forensic hospital, which needs nurses and mental health technicians in Tuscaloosa [16] [135], is the system's weakest link. Telehealth and the compacts ease the outpatient end; they do not help inpatient units. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0182 -->

<!-- section: d5 -->
## Domain 5. Medicines, Technology and Infrastructure

<!-- section: d5-scope -->
### Scope

This domain covers the physical and technical capacity behind services: hospital and crisis-center buildings, residential beds, the information systems that carry client records and claims, telehealth, broadband, and access to psychiatric medications. Infrastructure decisions are slow and expensive; they set the ceiling on what the other domains can deliver for a decade. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0198 -->

<!-- section: d5-facilities -->
### Facilities

**State hospitals.** Bryce Hospital was rebuilt in 2014 on a new Tuscaloosa campus [147]. Taylor Hardin Secure Medical Facility, the forensic hospital, has grown from 140 to 225 beds; about 200 were in use by August 2026, with the remaining 25 awaiting a plumbing repair that the Commissioner expected in the first week of October 2026 [32]. No report that those beds had opened was found as of 3 October 2026. The Mary Starke Harper Geriatric Psychiatry Center serves older adults [16]. Together with the adolescent unit at East Alabama Medical Center, the three hospitals served 637 people in FY2025 [16]. State hospital operations cost about $123 million in FY2025 [16]. <!-- b: B0200 -->

**Crisis infrastructure.** The six crisis centers [14] and the pediatric unit [41] are the state's newest buildings. ACES's preliminary evaluation of September 2025 found capital costs ranged from $3.3 million to $12.6 million per center and that FY2024 utilization averaged 27 percent — meaning the physical capacity exists to absorb growth before new construction [14]. The Department's response, printed with the evaluation, questions how utilization and average daily bed costs were calculated and says "most centers function at capacity most days" [14]; at a public meeting that September the Commissioner and her staff pushed back on the analysis, saying the Department cannot turn over protected health information [19]. The Legislature's FY2027 line of $36 million is for the "Existing Six Crisis Diversion Centers" [18]; this report reads it as operating money, not capital. <!-- b: B0201 -->

**Beds.** Involuntary bed capacity — 698 at the start of FY2025, 13.7 per 100,000 [16]; 771, or 15.1 per 100,000, in the Department's January 2026 count, against the 1,550 beds (30 per 100,000) that the Department, citing the Treatment Advocacy Center, treats as the minimum [30] — is the system's defining infrastructure gap. The Department prices the 779 further beds at $226.4 million among its five-year financial goals [30]; the $590 million estimate for replacing 787 beds, given in August 2026 at a Joint Mental Health Task Force meeting by Rep. Rex Reynolds, the House General Fund budget chair [32] [33], is a construction-only figure that the Commissioner said cannot be met from current revenues [32]. The two are separate estimates, not reconciled in the sources. Residential step-down capacity (the residential categories in ADMH's contract service delivery manual) is contracted rather than built [108]; no statewide count of those beds was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0202 -->

**Detoxification.** ADMH certifies 104 detox beds statewide, including River Crossing in Huntsville (16 beds) [16]. <!-- b: B0203 -->

**Corrections.** The 4,000-bed Governor Kay Ivey Correctional Complex in Elmore County, built at about $1.25 billion with specialized medical and mental-health units, is due to be completed by 15 October 2026; the Department of Corrections flagged a "reasonable concern" of delay in July 2026 (Section 10) [148]. No report of completion, opening or a revised date was found as of 3 October 2026. <!-- b: B0204 -->

<!-- section: d5-information-systems -->
### Information systems

ADMH runs two provider-reporting systems: the **Alabama Community Services Information System (ACSIS)** for mental-illness and CCBHC providers — client, episode, staff and 837P billing submissions — and the **Alabama Substance Abuse Information System (ASAIS)** for substance-use providers [149] [150]. The 2025 edition of this report used several other names for these systems; none was correct. The Department's certification process moved to an automated platform (ACAP) in November 2025 [16]. <!-- b: B0206 -->

**One Health Record**, the Alabama Medicaid health information exchange, announced in April 2023 that it planned to join the eHealth Exchange QHIN under the federal TEFCA framework [151], and reports that in 2023 it was the "first HIE to successfully exchange data across the TEFCA QHIN framework" [45]; as of December 2025 it connected 57 hospitals (16 more in progress), more than 800 clinics and 15 FQHC, rural-health, health-information-network and CMHC organizations (more than 300 sites). ADMH's own connection was listed as a work in progress ("ADMH W-I-P") [45]. CCBHCs must report federally specified outcome measures; ADMH's board minutes reference a Netsmart system for that purpose [26]. <!-- b: B0207 -->

Clinical data sharing remains constrained by federal substance-use confidentiality rules (42 CFR Part 2) [152] and by the fact that each CMHC runs its own electronic health record [153]. <!-- b: B0208 -->

<!-- section: d5-telehealth-and-broadband -->
### Telehealth and broadband

Alabama Medicaid reimburses live-video and audio-only telehealth at parity with in-person visits and allows patients to be served at home (originating sites receive a $20 fee; the home does not); the Center for Connected Health Policy finds no private-payer telehealth parity law [88]. The CCBHC provider manual permits telehealth for eligible services on a HIPAA-compliant platform with documented client preference, following Medicaid's telehealth chapter [10]. 988 text and chat are available [9], and a federal rule effective October 2025 requires nationwide carriers to georoute 988 texts within 18 months (36 months for smaller carriers), so that texts reach the nearest 988 center [154] [155]. <!-- b: B0210 -->

Broadband is the constraint on telehealth's reach. About 87.5 percent of Alabama households had a broadband subscription in 2020–2024, leaving one in eight without [133]. Alabama's final BEAD proposal, approved in March 2026, commits nearly $460 million across 63 projects to reach roughly 92,000 unserved locations; 71 percent of the projects will use fiber and 24 percent low-earth-orbit satellite [156]. <!-- b: B0211 -->

<!-- section: d5-medicines -->
### Medicines

Psychiatric medication for people in the public system is paid for by Medicaid where they are enrolled and otherwise through the state's Indigent Drug Program, dispensed through the community mental health centers, and manufacturer assistance [157]; the FY2025 report does not publish a prescriptions count. Medication-assisted treatment for opioid use disorder is a Medicaid benefit [158] [159], and ADMH distributed 48,179 boxes of naloxone between 1 November 2024 and 31 October 2025 (40,733 kits in fiscal year 2025) [76]. The Prescription Drug Monitoring Program is run by the Department of Public Health's Pharmacy Division under Code of Alabama §20-2-210 [160]. The 2025 edition's statement that Alabama had enacted prescriptive authority for psychologists was incorrect; Alabama is not among the nine jurisdictions that have enacted it [161], and no such Alabama statute was found. <!-- b: B0213 -->

Medication continuity at transitions — hospital discharge, jail release, loss of coverage — is, in this report's reading, a reason the CCBHC model's provision for up to 72 hours of follow-up after a crisis response matters [105]. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0214 -->

<!-- section: d5-transportation -->
### Transportation

Alabama Medicaid operates a Non-Emergency Transportation program [47] [162]. As of ALDOT's 2023 study, twenty-eight rural (Section 5311) transit providers serve 58 of 67 counties; nine counties, including several urban ones served by city systems, have no rural provider [47]. The only mental-health-specific transport support identified is the transportation voucher within the IPS supported-employment program [128]. <!-- b: B0216 -->

<!-- section: d5-continuum-lens -->
### Continuum lens

Alabama's infrastructure investment since 2020 has gone to the crisis and forensic ends of the continuum [14] [32]. The middle — residential step-down, supported housing, outpatient clinic space in rural counties — has no capital program that was found for this report. Telehealth and broadband are the cheapest way to extend the outpatient end; they do nothing for beds. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0218 -->

## Domain 7. Equity, Inclusion and Cultural Responsiveness

<!-- section: d7-scope -->
### Scope

Equity asks who the system reaches, who it misses, and whether the care people receive fits their language, culture, disability and circumstances. In Alabama the relevant dimensions are race, rurality, income and insurance, language and hearing, veteran status, age, and involvement with the justice and child-welfare systems. This domain reports what the data show and names the programs built for specific communities; it makes no clinical claims and takes no policy position. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0269 -->

<!-- section: d7-who-alabama-is -->
### Who Alabama is

Alabama's population of 5.19 million is 25.9 percent Black and 6.5 percent Hispanic [133]; 42 percent of residents live in rural areas, among the ten highest shares nationally [134]. In 2024, 15.2 percent of residents lived below the poverty line (child poverty 20.2 percent) [179], and the state's median household income of $63,999 in the 2020–2024 five-year estimate is well below the national figure [133]. The Poarch Band of Creek Indians, the state's only federally recognized tribe [180], operates its own health department with behavioral-health services at the Buford L. Rolin Health Clinic in Atmore [181]. Alabama is home to roughly 300,000–377,000 veterans, depending on the count used [182] [183]. <!-- b: B0271 -->

<!-- section: d7-what-the-data-show -->
### What the data show

**Race.** The public mental health system serves Black Alabamians at a higher share (38.3 percent of people served in the 2025 reporting year; 37.8 percent in 2024) than their share of the population (25.9 percent) [42] [43] [133], the reverse of what the 2025 edition of this report stated: its 17.4 percent was the national figure in the 2022 tables, not Alabama's [165]. No explanation was found in the federal tables or in the Department's published reports as of October 2026; plausible contributors are higher uninsurance and poverty rates, which route people to the public system, and the justice and crisis pathways that feed it. Whether outcomes differ by race is reported only in part (Domain 6): the federal tables break out consumer-survey results, homelessness and state-hospital readmissions by race, but not employment. In the 2025 tables, 84 percent of Black adults and 81 percent of White adults surveyed reported positive outcomes, and 2.9 percent of Black and 2.7 percent of White clients with a known living situation were homeless [42]. Outside mental health, the disparity is stark: Alabama's pregnancy-related mortality rate for Black women was 77.0 per 100,000 live births against 28.2 for White women in 2020–2021, the latest review to publish rates by race [170]. <!-- b: B0273 -->

**Rurality.** Fifty-five of 67 counties are deemed rural [111] [184], and 42 percent of Alabamians live in rural areas [134]. In this report's reading, rural residents face the longest distances to the six crisis centers [12] [111], the thinnest provider supply, nine counties with no rural transit provider, several of them urban counties with city systems [47], and the largest broadband gaps (87.5 percent of households statewide have a broadband subscription) [133]. The five rural crisis-care programs, first funded in FY2021 [111] and carried at $6 million a year in the General Fund, and the $5 million child-and-adolescent mobile-crisis pilot, in the budget since at least FY2025, are the state's current response [20] [18]. <!-- b: B0274 -->

**Income and insurance.** Among Alabama adults with any mental illness, 14.1 percent are uninsured (U.S. 9.2 percent), and 30.0 percent of adults reporting 14 or more mentally unhealthy days could not see a doctor because of cost (U.S. 26.6 percent) [35]. The Medicaid coverage gap is put at up to 155,000 people by Alabama Arise [36] [37]; KFF's July 2026 estimate is 86,000 uninsured adults in the gap itself and 147,000 who would become eligible if the state expanded [38]. About 95,000 people left marketplace coverage in 2026 [36] [39]. These figures are the equity face of the financing gap in Domain 2 and Section 12. <!-- b: B0275 -->

**Children.** Alabama ranks 50th of 51 on Mental Health America's youth measures; 66.4 percent of Alabama youth with major depression received no treatment, the highest rate in the country, and 18 percent of youth with private insurance had plans that did not cover mental health (U.S. 8.6 percent) [35] (Section 9). <!-- b: B0276 -->

**Justice involvement.** People waiting in county jails for psychiatric evaluation or treatment — 291 in July 2026 [32] — are the population whose access to care is governed by a federal consent decree [11]; ADOC's mental health caseload was about 3,400 of 19,500 people in custody when the _Braggs_ suit was filed in 2014 (Section 10) [69]. <!-- b: B0277 -->

<!-- section: d7-programs-built-for-specific-communities -->
### Programs built for specific communities

- **Deaf and hard-of-hearing Alabamians.** ADMH's Office of Deaf Services provides linguistically specific care through regionally based clinicians and communication-access staff [52] [185]. <!-- b: B0279 -->

- **Veterans.** Alabama has four VA medical centers (Birmingham, Tuscaloosa, Montgomery and Tuskegee) and four Vet Centers; a 2024 training deck prepared for ADMH lists 17 community-based outpatient clinics (three of them in Georgia) plus two outpatient clinics [183]; the Veterans Crisis Line is reached through 988 (press 1) [9]. ADMH's veterans mental-health plan was funded at $12 million in 2025 [186], the Alabama Veterans Resource Center opened in Montgomery in February 2026 under SB70 [187] [188] [189], and the Houston Hunter Act created voluntary firearm-hold agreements after 139 Alabama veteran suicides in 2022 (more than 85 percent of Alabama veteran suicides involve a firearm) [186] [64]. Fox Army Health Center at Redstone Arsenal serves the active-duty community in North Alabama [190]. <!-- b: B0280 -->

- **Faith communities.** More than 400 faith leaders have been certified as Faith-Based Support Specialists since 2022 through the Agency for Substance Abuse Prevention, which ADMH funds in part [141]. <!-- b: B0281 -->

- **Mothers and infants.** Alabama Medicaid covers twelve months of postpartum care [106], an extension that KFF's tracker of 15 July 2026 lists as implemented under a state plan amendment approved on 13 January 2023 [191]; ADMH's infant and early-childhood programs support young children and the adults who care for them [192], and the Postpartum Support International Alabama chapter addresses perinatal mental health (Section 9) [193]. <!-- b: B0282 -->

- **Language access.** ADMH's program rules require certified providers to offer free language assistance to people with limited English proficiency or who are deaf, and allow telephone or video interpreting for spoken languages [194]; no ADMH language-access plan or CLAS-standards assessment was found as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0283 -->

- **Tribal.** The Poarch Band's Tribal Health Improvement Plan (2015–2020) set diabetes, obesity and hypertension as its priorities; it lists behavioral health among the tribal clinic's services and mental-health awareness among its community recommendations [195]. Coordination with the state system was not found in public documents as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0284 -->

- **LGBTQ+ Alabamians.** Supportive programming exists through community organizations such as Birmingham's Magic City Acceptance Center [196]; no state data or program specific to this population was found as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0285 -->

## Domain 8. Community Engagement, Education and Advocacy

<!-- section: d8-scope -->
### Scope

This domain covers how the public shapes the system: awareness and anti-stigma education, training of lay and professional responders, the organizations that carry the voice of people with lived experience and their families, regional coalitions, the press, and the advocacy that moves budgets and law. ADMH's fifth strategic priority — Awareness and Knowledge — commits the Department to giving Alabamians information that raises awareness and connects them to services [40]; this report reads that as recognizing public engagement as a system function, not an add-on. <!-- b: B0303 -->

<!-- section: d8-who-engages -->
### Who engages

**NAMI Alabama** and its eleven listed affiliates (one still forming) run family and peer education and support groups [197] [199], and its CIT Division coordinates the state's regional Crisis Intervention Team programs (Section 10); the Division's 2024–2026 strategic plan organizes its regional coordinators into six regions matching the boundaries of the six crisis centers [145]. **The Alabama Suicide Prevention and Resources Coalition (ASPARC)**, which began as a task force chosen to help the Department of Public Health with suicide-prevention strategy and is now a nonprofit, works to prevent suicide through education, advocacy and awareness and trains people in suicide prevention [200]; it is the state coalition listed alongside ADMH's suicide prevention coordinator and the 2021–2026 state plan [122]. **The Alabama Council of Community Mental Health Boards** (which also uses the name Alabama Council for Behavioral Healthcare) represents its members, 22 community mental health providers by its own count [201]. **The Alabama Disabilities Advocacy Program** protects rights [202] and monitors the _Hunter_ decree [11]. **Alabama Arise**, through its **Cover Alabama** campaign, a coalition of more than 130 organizations, advocates coverage expansion [36] [203]. **VOICES for Alabama's Children** compiles child well-being data [172] [174]. **Regional coalitions** — including the North Alabama Mental Health Coalition, formed in 2015, which meets monthly and brings together mental-health providers, law enforcement, local officials, probate judges, advocates and social-service agencies [204] — connect these statewide bodies to local practice; the 2025 edition of this report recommended creating "a united Mental Health Coalition" without noting that such coalitions already exist. <!-- b: B0305 -->

**Public education and training.** Each May ADMH joins organizations across the state in observing Mental Health Awareness Month, with events such as its annual Strike Out Stigma game and Capitol Art Showcase [205]; ADMH's Prevention Day drew about 100 attendees in April 2025 [16]. Mental Health First Aid, which ADMH, NAMI Alabama and more than 90 certified instructors offer to audiences that include houses of faith, schools and human-resource staff [206], and the Faith-Based Support Specialist program, an addiction-recovery training that has certified more than 400 faith leaders since 2022 [141], extend recognition and referral skills into congregations and workplaces. Schools now host both CMHC services and Mental Health Services Coordinators (Section 9) [119] [120]. <!-- b: B0306 -->

**Press.** The Alabama Reflector, Alabama Political Reporter, Alabama Daily News and regional outlets have covered the crisis-center evaluation, the forensic waitlist, the prison litigation and the coverage gap in detail during 2025–2026 [19] [32] [116] [70] [72] [37] [39]; this report cites them where they report primary documents. <!-- b: B0307 -->

### What advocacy has achieved and where it stands

The crisis-system build-out of 2020–2024 is the clearest case of advocacy, data and executive leadership aligning: local advocates pressed for a crisis center (in North Alabama, a 2019 petition signed by probate judges, police chiefs and sheriffs) [204], the Department made a specific request, $18 million for three crisis centers [207], the Governor made it a priority and the Legislature funded it, beginning with the FY2021 budget [208] [14]. # Part III. Systems Beyond the Department

<!-- section: s9 -->
## Section 9. Children, Youth and Families: A System of Care

<!-- section: s9-scope-and-frame -->
### Scope and frame

Children's mental health in Alabama is delivered by at least six public systems — the Department of Mental Health, schools, Medicaid, child welfare, juvenile justice and early-childhood education — plus pediatric medicine. The national System of Care framework, adopted by the federal government since the 1980s and updated in 2010 [7] and again in 2021 [210], holds that services for children should be family-driven and youth-guided, community-based, culturally and linguistically competent, and coordinated across agencies, with early identification, individualized planning and smooth transitions to adulthood [7]. The 2021 update keeps each of these values and principles, with some rewording (the first value is now "Family and Youth Driven"), and adds principles on trauma-informed care, health–mental health integration, a public health approach and mental health equity [210]. This section describes Alabama's pieces against that frame. Child and Family Services is the second of ADMH's five strategic priorities [40]. <!-- b: B0329 -->

<!-- section: s9-where-alabama-stands -->
### Where Alabama stands

Alabama ranks **50th of 51** on Mental Health America's composite youth measures. An estimated 18.9 percent of Alabama youth (about 75,000) had a major depressive episode in the latest data — close to the national rate — but **66.4 percent of them received no treatment, the highest share in the country** (U.S. 50.8 percent). Eighteen percent of Alabama youth with private insurance had plans that did not cover mental health (U.S. 8.6 percent), also the worst in the country; 13.5 percent reported serious thoughts of suicide (U.S. 12.9 percent); and only 1.5 per 1,000 students had an emotional-disturbance designation for special education, the lowest reported rate in the country (two states reported no data) [35]. Teen (15–19) suicide was 9.9 per 100,000 in 2021–2023, slightly below the national rate [211]. Alabama's Youth Risk Behavior Survey was last conducted in 2021, and in 2023 the state returned the federal funds for it [171]; no later state survey was found as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0331 -->

<!-- section: s9-the-department-s-children-s-system -->
### The Department's children's system

ADMH created the **Office of Child and Family Services** in October 2023 [212], consolidating child and adolescent mental-illness treatment, adolescent substance-use treatment, autism services, infant and early-childhood mental health, early intervention, intensive in-home services, prevention and school-based mental health [131] [212]. In FY2025 the public system served **31,847 adolescents for mental illness and an estimated 4,506 for substance use** [16], compared with 29,796 and 2,739 in FY2024 [115]; because ADMH footnotes its FY2025 substance-use figures as estimated after a system transformation, the two years may not be like-for-like [16]. Five child and adolescent mobile-crisis teams at three centers — AltaPointe, JBS and WellStone [111] — served **936 young people**, the first year that count appeared in the Department's annual report [16]; WellStone's 24-bed **pediatric crisis unit** (ages 12–18) in Huntsville — the first in the state, opened in late September 2024 — performed 520 evaluations [41] [115] [16]. Adult crisis centers may not admit children [14], and the Huntsville unit is the only pediatric crisis unit on the Department's list of crisis centers [12]. The General Fund has carried a $5 million child-and-adolescent mobile-crisis pilot line since at least FY2025, unchanged in FY2027 [20] [18]. <!-- b: B0333 -->

Services for children with serious emotional disturbance — crisis, outpatient, outreach, day treatment and residential care — are offered by the state's treatment providers, with at least one of these offered in every county; intensive home-based services are a separate program of the office [131]. Infant and early-childhood programs include consulting therapists in child-care and pediatric settings; **PATHS** (Pediatric Access to Telemental Health Services), a behavioral-health consultation line for primary-care practices run with Children's of Alabama under a federal grant; and **PPEER**, a 2024–2028 federally funded program for children up to age 12 who are at risk for or engaged with child protective services in Jefferson, Jackson and Mobile counties [192] [130]. ADMH's page links a Child and Family Services resource directory, a file uploaded in August 2025 [131]. Eleven certified providers treat adolescent substance use [131]. <!-- b: B0334 -->

The 2025 edition of this report referred to wraparound and therapeutic-foster-care programs under names that could not be confirmed; therapeutic foster care in Alabama is a DHR-contracted service [213], and this edition does not attribute programs to ADMH that its own pages do not list. <!-- b: B0335 -->

<!-- section: s9-schools -->
### Schools

Schools are the largest point of contact. Two programs run in parallel: <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0337 -->

- **ADMH–ALSDE School-Based Mental Health Collaboration** (since 2010) brings CMHC services into **122 of Alabama's school systems** through all 19 centers [119], funded at $5.5 million in the FY2027 Education Trust Fund [58]. <!-- b: B0338 -->

- **ALSDE Mental Health Services Coordinators**: local education agencies that apply receive about $40,000 a year for a coordinator who links students to services [120]. The sources disagree on the program's FY2026 funding: ALSDE's memo says the FY2026 ETF (Act 2025-270) appropriated $6.36 million [120], while the Legislature's FY2027 ETF sheet shows the line at $4.69 million budgeted for FY2026 and $9.25 million in the FY2027 ETF [58]. It began as a 102-district pilot in 2020–21 and was due to reach every district in 2022–23 [214], when Act 2022-442 required each local education agency to employ a coordinator [60] [121]. <!-- b: B0339 -->

Since 2022 (Act 2022-442), schools must give annual written notice, keep mental-health records separate from academic records and obtain **written opt-in consent** from a parent before mental-health services, surveys or assessments, at first for students under 14 [121] [60]; since 1 October 2025, SB101 (Act 2025-455) raises that age to 16, so that students under 16 cannot consent to ongoing counseling on their own [59] [60]. The statute excepts an imminent threat to the health of the student or others, suspected abuse, neglect or exploitation, and an immediate need for grief counseling [59], and ALSDE's guidance treats instruction, advisement and occasional interventions as outside the opt-in requirement [60] [121]. The practical effect on school-based service volume is not reported: no before-and-after count of school-based services was found in the sources read for this section as of October 2026. Alabama's school-counselor ratio was about 401 students per counselor in 2024–25 (398 in 2023–24) against a recommended 250 [215] [216]. <!-- b: B0340 -->

<!-- section: s9-child-welfare -->
### Child welfare

The Department of Human Resources had **5,900 children in out-of-home care** in May 2025, 576 of them with relatives. In its federal case review, DHR rated mental and behavioral health needs as a strength in 48 percent of cases reviewed (11 of 23) in FY2024, and it funds a psychotropic-medication review team for children in foster care from adoption savings [217]. <!-- section: s10 -->
## Section 10. The Justice Interface

<!-- section: s10-scope-and-frame -->
### Scope and frame

In Alabama, the justice system is a regular point of contact for people with mental illness: Lee County's sheriff says some sheriffs describe their jails as a "de facto center" for people with mental-health conditions, and the director of Alabama's Crisis Intervention Team program estimates that 20 to 30 percent of the calls law enforcement fields involve a person in a mental-health crisis [6]. This section follows the **Sequential Intercept Model**, which maps the points at which a person can be diverted from the justice system into care: Intercept 0 (community and crisis services), 1 (law enforcement), 2 (initial detention and court hearings), 3 (jails and courts), 4 (reentry) and 5 (community corrections) [4] [5]. Alabama's forensic and prison mental-health systems both operate under federal court oversight [11] [69], which makes this the area where the state's obligations are most explicit. <!-- b: B0368 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-0-community-and-crisis -->
### Intercept 0 — Community and crisis response

The crisis system described in Domain 3 is the first intercept: four in-state 988 centers [16] [9], fourteen mobile crisis teams [13] and six crisis diversion centers that accept law-enforcement drop-offs around the clock [12]. **Co-responder programs** pair officers with clinicians: in Huntsville, Huntsville Police with WellStone, funded in 2021, had two master's-level clinicians by early 2022 [112]; in Mobile, the police announced a one-precinct pilot with AltaPointe in May 2022 [113]. No published figures on the current staffing or outcomes of these programs were found as of October 2026. Under a 2022 law (HB70, Act 2022-202) that took effect in July 2022, officers may transport a person in crisis to a crisis center rather than jail [225] [226] [227]. <!-- b: B0370 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-1-law-enforcement -->
### Intercept 1 — Law enforcement

**Crisis Intervention Team (CIT)** training in Alabama is coordinated by NAMI Alabama's CIT Division, under a statewide administrative coordinator, named in the Division's 2024–2026 plan as John (Johnny) Hollingsworth [145] [144], with a $1 million General Fund line through ADMH in FY2026 and FY2027 [20] [18]. The 2024–2026 strategic plan started from five regional programs — WellStone with Huntsville Police, JBS with the Jefferson County Sheriff, Hope Pointe with Tuscaloosa Police, Carastar with Opelika Police (Montgomery under development) and AltaPointe with Mobile Police and the Baldwin County Sheriff — and aims for six regions aligned with the six crisis centers, for the International Association of Chiefs of Police "One Mind" campaign (all sworn officers trained in mental-health response, at least 20 percent CIT-trained), and for APOST credit for all CIT courses [145]. The Peace Officers' Standards and Training Commission has listed a two-day CIT class for 16 continuing-education units [142]; it requires 12 hours of continuing education a year with no mental-health subject mandated [143], and it raised the basic academy's Mental Health Awareness block from four to eight hours from January 2018 [228]. NAMI Alabama's CIT pages give no statewide count of CIT-trained officers; ADMH's July 2024 budget presentation reported "1375 plus CIT trained officers" [135], and no current or agency-level count was found as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0372 -->

**Lethal means.** The Houston Hunter Act (SB40, 2025) created voluntary firearm-hold agreements with licensed dealers and the SAFER program, against a background of 139 veteran suicides in Alabama in 2022; more than 85 percent of the state's veteran suicides involve a firearm [64]. <!-- b: B0373 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-2-initial-detention-and -->
### Intercept 2 — Initial detention and initial court hearings

**Jail screening.** No statewide standard for mental-health screening at booking was found for Alabama as of October 2026. Morgan County's jail, for example, employs two licensed counselors who logged 6,338 inmate visits in 2025, and its Sheriff's Office says mental-health crisis issues occur in about half of inmates [229]. No statewide count of jail suicides was found as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0375 -->

**Civil commitment.** Under Code of Alabama §22-52 any person may petition the probate court; the standard for inpatient commitment is clear and convincing evidence of mental illness and a real and present threat of substantial harm to self or others, together with findings that the person will continue to deteriorate without treatment and cannot make a rational decision about treatment, and the court must order the least restrictive alternative necessary and available; initial orders run up to 150 days [66]. Outpatient commitment exists where a designated facility consents [66]. No annual count of commitments was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0376 -->

**Specialty courts.** SB200 (Act 2025-183) renamed drug courts "accountability courts," extended eligibility to defendants with mental illness and to veterans, and directed the Administrative Office of Courts to set statewide policy, effective 1 October 2025 [63] [62]; HB487, passed in April 2026, directs $3 million of opioid-settlement funds to the AOC for specialty courts [92] [90]. Before SB200, mental-health and veterans courts were local initiatives: Cullman County's circuit added a mental health court to its drug and veterans court dockets in 2022 [230], and Shelby County's district attorney's office lists a mental health court and a veterans court [231]; no count of the circuits now operating them was found as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0377 -->

**Regional boards.** SB236 (Act 2025-366, effective 1 October 2025) requires the executive committee of a regional (310) mental health authority board with more than sixteen directors to include a sheriff (or the sheriff's representative) and a probate judge where those officials serve on the board (the officials may opt out), formalizing the justice system's seat at the community mental health table [61] [62]. <!-- b: B0378 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-3-jails-and-courts-competency -->
### Intercept 3 — Jails and courts: competency and forensic care

This is Alabama's most litigated intercept. Under the 2018 _Hunter v. Beshear_ consent decree (now captioned _Hunter v. Boswell_), the state must evaluate and restore defendants found incompetent within set times [11] (60 days for evaluation and 30 days for admission to restoration, as the Associated Press describes the decree's terms [116]); the decree's inpatient-restoration term, extended in March 2025, runs to 31 January 2027, with the Alabama Disabilities Advocacy Program, an associational plaintiff, monitoring compliance [11]. The **forensic waitlist** — people waiting in county jails for psychiatric evaluation or treatment [32] — was 273 in August 2025 (the Associated Press's count of men waiting for Taylor Hardin) [116], 327 in January 2026 and **291 in July 2026**, against a Department goal of 100 by the end of 2026 [32]; in 2025 the average wait exceeded a year and more than thirty people had waited more than two years [116]. <!-- b: B0380 -->

The Department's response has four parts: the expansion of Taylor Hardin from 140 to 225 beds [32] [115] (about 200 of the 225 in use in August 2026, with the last 25 awaiting a plumbing repair then expected in the first week of October 2026 [32]; no report that they had opened was found as of 3 October 2026); **jail-based competency restoration**, including a telehealth program that had restored 32 people between summer 2025 and August 2026 [32] (the Department's jail-based program had 31 people enrolled and 23 completed as of September 2025 [16]); forensic navigators who work the list [32]; and outpatient forensic evaluations contracted to JBS under the Office of Forensic Mental Health Services [232]. The Commissioner told the board in August 2024 that only about half of Taylor Hardin's technician and nursing positions were filled [136] [116]. <!-- b: B0381 -->

**Stepping Up Alabama**, the state's version of the national county jail initiative, funded eight county sites through four CMHCs in its fourth round of grants (sites starting between January and July 2021), with awards originally capped at $50,000, and screened 6,926 people in jails, 261 of them positive for serious mental illness [233]; a map ADMH posted in April 2026 shows Stepping Up in 48 counties and 19 without [234]. The 2025 edition of this report attributed Stepping Up to ADMH as a state program; it is a national initiative sponsored by the National Association of Counties, the American Psychiatric Association Foundation and the Council of State Governments Justice Center that ADMH has administered locally [46] [233]. <!-- b: B0382 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-4-reentry -->
### Intercept 4 — Reentry

**Prisons.** In _Braggs v. Commissioner, Alabama Department of Corrections_ (the Eleventh Circuit's caption; the case is now styled _Braggs v. Lovelace_), a federal court in 2017 found mental health care in Alabama prisons "horrendously inadequate" and in violation of the Eighth Amendment [69] [71]. On 24 June 2026 the Eleventh Circuit affirmed the liability finding and the system-wide injunction but reversed the specific correctional-staffing mandate (the state had about 1,600 qualifying officers against about 3,600 required under the order), some suicide-resistant cell requirements and the application of relief to Tutwiler, and returned those questions to the district court [69] [70]. When the suit was filed in 2014, the record put ADOC's mental-health caseload at about 3,400 of roughly 19,500 people in custody [69]. The U.S. Department of Justice's separate suit, which alleges unconstitutional conditions in the men's prisons, is set for trial in November 2026 after a government motion for summary judgment in September [72]. ADOC announced on 29 April 2026 that it had terminated its $1.06 billion medical and mental-health contract with YesCare, which was to run through September 2027, and said it was working to execute an emergency agreement with NaphCare, with the transition set for 3 May 2026 (the release's headline describes a 24-month agreement) [235] [236]. The 4,000-bed Elmore County complex, which includes a 720-bed medical and mental-health area, was due to be completed by 15 October 2026, with ADOC warning in July of possible delay [148]; no report of completion or of a new date was found as of 3 October 2026. At least 277 people died in Alabama prisons in 2024 [237] and at least 202 in 2025 [238]. <!-- b: B0384 -->

**Reentry programs.** The Bureau of Pardons and Paroles operates the Perry County PREP Center in Uniontown [239] [77], announced in November 2025 a CARE Center for women in Thomasville, for an initial cohort of 20, to open in spring 2026 [240] (no report that it had opened was found as of 3 October 2026, and the Bureau's locations page did not list it [241]), opened a new skills facility at the PREP Center in September 2026 [242], and runs five Day Reporting Centers (Birmingham, Huntsville, Mobile, Montgomery, Tuscaloosa) with contracted mental-health and substance-use treatment [243], along with six "Day Reporting Center Lite" sites (Baldwin, Fort Payne, Jasper, Opelika, Sand Mountain, Calera) [241]. A Unite Us referral pilot with ADMH and the Alabama Department of Veterans Affairs, funded from opioid settlements, began at about five of the day-reporting centers in early 2026 [244]. The _Reentry Alabama_ plan, developed with the Council of State Governments Justice Center [77], and the Reentry Task Force created by SJR80 (2025) [77] and chaired by the Bureau's director [245] are the statewide coordinating instruments; in September 2026 the task force advanced a proposed recidivism definition for a vote at its next meeting [245]. No approved or pending Medicaid reentry waiver for Alabama was identified: the state is not mentioned in KFF's August 2024 review of such waivers or in the text of its waiver tracker as of 30 September 2026 [246] [247] (Appendix D). Alabama law provides that people held in state prisons and county jails have their Medicaid eligibility suspended, not terminated [248]. <!-- b: B0385 -->

<!-- section: s10-intercept-5-community-corrections-and -->
### Intercept 5 — Community corrections and parole

Thirty-eight community corrections programs cover 52 counties; fifteen counties have none, on ADOC's map dated 1 April 2023 [249]. The parole grant rate was 16 percent in November 2025 and 17 percent for the fiscal year to date, down from 29 percent a year earlier [250]; the Bureau's later monthly reports show 23 percent in June, 29 percent in July and 12 percent in August 2026, with 20 percent for the fiscal year through August [251] [252]; the Bureau's director reports recidivism falling from 34 to 27 percent [245]. No published figures on dedicated mental-health supervision caseloads were found as of October 2026. <!-- b: B0387 -->

## Section 11. Housing, Social Determinants and Community Supports

<!-- section: s11-scope-and-frame -->
### Scope and frame

Mental health outcomes depend on conditions outside the clinic: housing, income and work, transport, connectivity, food, education and social connection. Healthy People 2030 groups these into five domains of social determinants — economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context [8]. This section describes Alabama's systems in each, with a focus on housing and homelessness, where the mental health system's own gap is clearest. <!-- b: B0403 -->

<!-- section: s11-neighborhood-and-built-environment -->
### Neighborhood and built environment: housing and homelessness

**The count.** Alabama's 2024 Point-in-Time count found 4,601 people experiencing homelessness [254] [255], a 39 percent increase on 2023 [255] but still one of the lowest rates in the country at 8.9 per 10,000 residents (U.S. 22.6) [256]. The 2025 count found 4,137 people, about 10 percent fewer, according to the National Alliance to End Homelessness's fact sheet on HUD's 2025 count and HUD's own state report [257] [258]; nationally, overall homelessness fell 3 percent from 2024 to 2025 while chronic homelessness has risen 81 percent since 2013 [259]. HUD's state report for the 2025 count lists 502 of the people counted in Alabama as severely mentally ill [258]; in the public mental health system, 2.7 percent of people served (all ages) and 4.1 percent of adults served were homeless at the 2025 reporting point, up from 1.9 and 3.0 percent in 2024 [42] [43]. <!-- b: B0405 -->

**The system.** Nine HUD Continuums of Care plan the local homelessness response and set priorities for federal Continuum of Care funds [260] [261] [262]: AL-500 One Roof (Jefferson, St. Clair, Shelby); AL-501 Homeless Coalition of the Alabama Gulf Coast (Mobile, Baldwin); AL-502 Homeless Care Council of Northwest Alabama; **AL-503 North Alabama Coalition for the Homeless (Madison, Limestone, Morgan)**; AL-504 Mid-Alabama Coalition for the Homeless (Montgomery region); AL-505 Homeless Coalition of Northeast Alabama; AL-506 West Alabama Coalition for the Homeless (Tuscaloosa); AL-507 Alabama Rural Coalition for the Homeless (balance of state); and AL-508 Southeast Alabama Coalition for the Homeless [260]. ADECA passes through federal Emergency Solutions Grants [263] — $2.7 million to eleven grantees in December 2025, including $450,000 to Huntsville [264]. Jefferson County's housing authority wound down a CoC rental-assistance program that had once helped 585 people (370 households), in stages between October 2024 and May 2025, saying HUD had not provided enough funding to sustain it [265]. <!-- b: B0406 -->

**Mental-health housing.** ADMH funds a ladder of residential services through CMHC contracts — including supported housing, adult residential care homes, three-bed residential, semi-independent apartments, crisis residential and transitional-age residential [108] — but no statewide count of those beds was found in the Department's published reports as of October 2026; 259 people were in supported housing in the 2025 reporting year (240 in 2024) [42] [43]. A $500,000 ADMH contract with Navigate Affordable Housing Partners, which was approved to run through 30 September 2026, was to coordinate supportive-housing development for people with mental health problems who are homeless, in jails or in emergency departments [266]; no report of a renewal was found as of 3 October 2026. The Department's anti-eviction defense program is funded at $650,000 in the FY2027 General Fund, the level the Legislature kept after the Governor proposed halving it [18]. No state supportive-housing subsidy program was found as of October 2026; neither Section 811 project rental assistance through the Alabama Housing Finance Authority nor a Shelter Plus Care program could be confirmed by name (Appendix D). Birmingham's unsheltered-services roadmap includes 350 added shelter and transitional beds, with most of its strategies due by the end of 2026 [267]. <!-- b: B0407 -->

<!-- section: s11-economic-stability-income-work-and-food -->
### Economic stability: income, work and food

- **Poverty.** 15.2 percent of Alabamians (about 760,000) lived in poverty in 2024, the lowest rate in American Community Survey data going back to 2010; child poverty was 20.2 percent [179]; median household income was $63,999 in the 2020–2024 five-year estimate [133]. <!-- b: B0409 -->

- **Disability income.** In December 2025, 179,658 Alabamians received Social Security disability benefits as disabled workers and 138,832 received SSI (130,122 for disability), at an average SSI payment of about $736 a month (this report's division of the month's total payments by the number of recipients) [268]; a year earlier the counts were 185,048 and 140,811 [269]. <!-- b: B0410 -->

- **Work.** The Department of Rehabilitation Services' Vocational Rehabilitation Service served 45,793 people, of 72,236 served across all the Department's programs in its 2025 annual report, and closed 3,999 cases in competitive integrated employment [270]. The ADMH–ADRS **Individual Placement and Support** program, the evidence-based supported-employment model for people with serious mental illness [128] [271], expanded in February 2025 to five sites under a federal grant [128]; 234 people were in supported employment in the 2025 reporting year (205 in 2024), and 21.0 percent of adults served by the public system were employed against 28.8 percent nationally (21.7 and 28.9 percent in 2024) [42] [43]. <!-- b: B0411 -->

- **Food.** 17.8 percent of Alabamians were food-insecure in 2024 [272]. <!-- section: s12 -->
## Section 12. Payers, Coverage and the CCBHC Transition

<!-- section: s12-scope -->
### Scope

Who pays determines what gets delivered. This section describes Alabama's payer mix — Medicaid, commercial insurance, Medicare, the marketplace, federal and state direct funding — and the single largest change in how community mental health care is paid for in a generation: the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic model. <!-- framing --> <!-- b: B0436 -->

<!-- section: s12-the-coverage-picture -->
### The coverage picture

In 2024, 50.0 percent of Alabamians had employer coverage, 20.8 percent Medicare, 14.7 percent Medicaid or CHIP (each person counted under one coverage type), 6.2 percent individual-market coverage and 8.4 percent were uninsured [99]. Medicaid enrolls about 1.1 million people, half of them children, with the federal government paying 78 percent of the program's $7.9 billion, according to KFF's May 2025 fact sheet [96]. <!-- b: B0438 -->

Alabama is one of ten states that have not expanded Medicaid to low-income adults [281] [38]; its income limit for parents is about 18 percent of the federal poverty level [282], and childless adults without a disability are not eligible [283]. The result is a **coverage gap** — adults who earn too much for Alabama's narrow Medicaid eligibility and too little for marketplace subsidies [38] — put at **up to 155,000 people** by Alabama Arise in a September 2026 statement [36] [37]. KFF's July 2026 estimate, using 2024 data, is 86,000 uninsured adults in the gap itself and 147,000 who would become eligible if the state expanded [38]. Adult uninsurance in non-expansion states ran 16.2 percent in 2025 against 9 percent in expansion states [36] [37]. The "ALLHealth" proposal to use expansion funds for private coverage, unveiled by the Alabama Hospital Association and Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama in December 2024, has not been adopted [284] [38]. The 2025 federal budget law (H.R.1) ended, from January 2026, the temporary enhanced federal match offered to states that newly expand [80] and limits state provider taxes [81] [80]; were Alabama to expand, its 6 percent hospital provider tax would have to fall to 3.5 percent by FY2032, an estimated $118 million loss of hospital-tax revenue in that year [81]. The law's work requirements apply to expansion populations and so do not directly reach Alabama [81]. Separately, the expiry of enhanced marketplace subsidies at the end of 2025 cut Alabama's marketplace enrollment by about 95,000 people, or 23 percent, in 2026 [39]. <!-- b: B0439 -->

For people with mental illness the gap is visible in the national data: 14.1 percent of Alabama adults with any mental illness are uninsured (U.S. 9.2 percent), 14.2 percent of those with private insurance have plans that do not cover mental health (U.S. 9.6 percent), and 30 percent of adults reporting 14 or more mentally unhealthy days could not see a doctor because of cost (U.S. 26.6 percent) [35]. <!-- b: B0440 -->

<!-- section: s12-medicaid-behavioral-health -->
### Medicaid behavioral health

Alabama Medicaid is a fee-for-service program with no comprehensive managed-care plans [125]: benefits and provider qualifications are set by the state plan and ADMH certification and rates by the Agency's fee schedule [49], and the **Alabama Coordinated Health Network** provides care coordination in seven regions [126]. That is why the CCBHC rate could be introduced by state action and why there is no health plan to hold to parity on the Medicaid side. Medicaid covers rehabilitative mental-health and substance-use services under Administrative Code 560-X-47, delivered by ADMH-certified community mental health centers, certified substance-use providers and, for children, state agencies [49]. Behavioral-health-specific developments: <!-- b: B0442 -->

- **Mobile crisis** became a Medicaid benefit on 1 October 2023 under State Plan Amendment AL-23-0009, which qualifies for an enhanced federal match for 24/7 two-person teams with follow-up [75]. <!-- b: B0443 -->

- **Inpatient psychiatric care in institutions for mental disease** for adults aged 21–64 with serious mental illness is covered under a Section 1115 waiver running from 20 May 2022 to 19 May 2027, limited to facilities in Mobile and Baldwin counties — enrollees may use them regardless of their county of residence — with a 30-day average-stay cap [97]. <!-- b: B0444 -->

- **A substance-use disorder 1115 waiver**, submitted in July 2023, remains pending at CMS [98]. <!-- b: B0445 -->

- **Twelve months of postpartum coverage** has been in effect since October 2022 [106]. Alabama Medicaid's March 2023 alert called the extension a pilot [106]; KFF's tracker of 15 July 2026 lists it as implemented under a state plan amendment approved on 13 January 2023 [191]. <!-- b: B0446 -->

- **Telehealth** is reimbursed at parity for live video and audio-only, with the home as an allowed site [88]. <!-- b: B0447 -->

<!-- section: s12-the-ccbhc-transition -->
### The CCBHC transition

**What it is.** A Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic must deliver nine services — screening and assessment; person- and family-centered treatment planning; outpatient mental-health and substance-use treatment; primary-care screening and monitoring; targeted case management; psychiatric rehabilitation; peer and family support; intensive community-based care for veterans and service members; and 24/7 crisis services with mobile response and 72-hour follow-up — and is paid a **prospective daily rate** (Alabama uses PPS-1) for each Medicaid enrollee on any day a qualifying service is delivered, instead of fee-for-service [10] [105]. The rate is set for each clinic from a cost report of the total cost of operating the CCBHC [285], which is why it can fund crisis response, peer support and care coordination that fee schedules under-pay. <!-- b: B0449 -->

**Alabama's path.** ADMH received a $1 million federal planning grant in March 2023 to write state criteria [286], and on 4 June 2024 the federal government named Alabama one of ten states added to the Section 223 demonstration [25] [24] [74]. Only ADMH-designated CMHCs may apply, with quarterly entry points; a clinic may contract with designated collaborating organizations but must deliver at least 51 percent of encounters itself [10]. In the model so far: **WellStone** (Huntsville and Cullman) and **AltaPointe** (southwest and east Alabama), certified by the state effective July 2024 [26] [27] [28]; **Mountain Lakes Behavioral Healthcare** (Jackson, Marshall), approved by SAMHSA in October 2025; and **Highland Health Systems** (Calhoun, Cleburne), approved in April 2026 [27]. ADMH's CCBHC service directory also lists a fifth, South Central Alabama Mental Health, which the May 2026 release does not name [28], and the Department's board was told in November 2025 that five centers "will become CCBHCs in 2026" [56]. In August 2026 six providers — Integrea, Northwest Alabama Mental Health Center, Carastar, CarePath, Central Alabama Wellness and JBS — received $4.4 million in first-round Rural Health Transformation grants specifically to pursue certification [29]; a second round of 34 grants totaling nearly $55 million, to providers of all kinds and none described as being for CCBHC certification, followed on 1 October 2026 [104]. The Department's stated aim is a statewide transition [27] [287]; the 2025 edition of this report's phrase "convert all 19 CMHCs" does not appear in any ADMH source and is replaced here. <!-- b: B0450 -->

**Early results.** The first two CCBHCs served **30,275 people in FY2025** [16]. ADMH has adopted an outcomes platform for CCBHC reporting [26]. The Department's January 2026 budget presentation reports that at WellStone the wait for an initial evaluation fell from 18 days in January 2025 to less than one day in December 2025, and the wait for a first therapy session from 33 days to eight [30]. No clinical outcome measures — hospitalization, emergency use, employment, housing — were found in the Department's published reports for the Alabama demonstration as of October 2026 (Appendix D). <!-- b: B0451 -->

**Why it matters for the rest of the system.** CCBHC changes the financing logic of Domains 2 and 3. Crisis response and follow-up, now funded by General Fund lines the Legislature conditions and cuts [17] [20] [18], become Medicaid-reimbursable services inside a clinic's rate; a certified peer specialist's service is enough to trigger the daily payment, and case management is carried as a cost inside the rate [288] [285]; and the clinic, not the state, carries responsibility for the hand-offs between crisis, outpatient and recovery supports. The limit is the same as everywhere else in this report: the rate applies to Medicaid enrollees [105], and Alabama's uninsured adults — the coverage-gap population — are outside it. <!-- synthesis --> <!-- b: B0452 -->

### Commercial insurance, Medicare and other payers

**Blue Cross Blue Shield of Alabama** held 84 percent of the state's commercial market in 2024, with the second carrier at 6 percent; the American Medical Association's 2025 study ranks Alabama the least competitive health-insurance market in the country [100]. ParityTrack's Alabama page, last modified in 2021, reports no published mental-health parity enforcement actions in the state [101]. Medicare covers one in five Alabamians [99]. Employer assistance programs, TRICARE and the VA, which has four medical centers in the state [183], complete the picture; this report did not compile Alabama-specific figures for them. <!-- b: B0454 -->
