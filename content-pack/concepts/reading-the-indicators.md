## What it is

An indicator is a number that stands in for something you cannot see directly. Nobody can watch a mental health system "reach" people, so we count the people it served and divide by the population [42]. That fraction is the indicator. It is only as good as the choices packed inside it: who was counted on top, who was counted underneath, over which months, and by whom.

Those choices are easy to lose sight of, even for careful people. The 2025 edition of this report said that 17.4 percent of the people Alabama's public system served were Black. That was the national figure. Alabama's share was more than twice as high, and stood at 38.3 percent in 2025 [165] [42]. The same edition gave an employment rate of 45.5 percent, which was the share employed among clients in the labor force. Among all adult clients whose status was known it was 20.5 percent [165]. Neither number was invented. Each was a real figure answering a different question from the one the sentence asked.

Most of the comparisons in this report come from one place, the federal Uniform Reporting System tables. Every state files them as a condition of its mental health block grant, and the federal agency publishes them beside national figures [42]. Others come from organizations that rank states, from one-night counts, and from the Department's own annual report. Each kind has its own habits.

## How it works in Alabama

Start with the measure the report uses most. The penetration rate is the number of people the public system served for every 1,000 residents. Alabama's was 18.31 in 2025, the same as the year before, against 22.94 for the country [42]. It is tempting to read that as unmet need. The number will not bear it. People treated through private insurance are not in it, and a low rate could reflect less need, poorer access or simply a smaller public role.

Small numbers are the next trap. Alabama's 30-day readmission rate to state hospitals fell from 6.8 percent to 1.5 percent in a year, far below the national 9.0. Behind those percentages are five readmissions among 74 discharges, then one among 68 [42] [43]. Four people account for the whole change.

Rankings need a different kind of care. Mental Health America ranks 51 jurisdictions, and by its convention a rank near 51 is bad [35]. Alabama is 50th on its youth measures. Look underneath, though, and the picture is more specific. About as many Alabama young people have a major depressive episode as elsewhere, 18.9 percent. What is exceptional is that 66.4 percent of them got no treatment, the highest share in the country [35]. A rank tells you the order of the states and nothing about the distance between them, nor about which part of the measure is doing the work.

Then there is time. Alabama's fiscal year runs from October to September, and the federal reporting year covers the same months [301] [42]. Deaths are counted by calendar year. The same program can therefore produce two honest totals: the opioid council's report gives 48,179 boxes of naloxone distributed between November 2024 and October 2025, and 40,733 kits in fiscal year 2025 [76]. Some figures are also provisional. The council's 2025 overdose count of 1,052 is an early estimate, and its 2024 figure of at least 1,151 is a floor, so the pair should not be read as a decline [76].

One-night counts have their own limits. The homelessness figure comes from a count made by local coalitions on a single night: 4,601 people in 2024 and 4,137 in 2025 [254] [258]. The federal housing department itself cautions that "the reliability and consistency of the homeless counts may also vary" from one coalition to another [258].

Last, watch the words. "Rural" means 55 of 67 counties in one sentence and 42 percent of residents in another, and those are different measurements [111] [134]. A county "served" by a crisis center has been assigned to it, which is a statement about responsibility and not about the drive [12].

## How this report uses it

Domain 6 has the main table of federal measures and the list of what Alabama does not publish. Appendix B carries the baseline indicators. Wherever the report gives an Alabama figure it tries to give the comparison, the period and the source key beside it, so use them [42].

On the site's maps, apply the same habits. A shaded county tells you what the legend says and no more. If the legend says "assigned to a crisis center", it is not a map of access [12]. And when a number seems to settle an argument, look for its denominator first.
