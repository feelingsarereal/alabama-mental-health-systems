## What it is

Say "diversion" and most people picture a single moment, an officer deciding not to make an arrest. The Sequential Intercept Model starts from the observation that there are several such moments, spread over months or years, and that each one is in different hands. Three researchers, Mark Munetz, Patricia Griffin and Henry Steadman, developed it in the early 2000s [5]. The federal center that promotes it calls it "a conceptual model to inform community-based responses to the involvement of people with mental and substance use disorders in the criminal justice system" [5].

The model lays the justice system out as a path and marks six places along it. They are numbered from 0 to 5: community services, law enforcement, initial detention and first court hearings, jails and courts, reentry, and community corrections [4]. At each place there is something that can be done. A mobile crisis team can go out where a patrol car would have gone. A brief screen at the booking desk, which the federal brochure says can be given to everyone by staff with no clinical training, can flag a person before the first hearing. A treatment court can supervise someone through care. A jail can plan a person's release so that medication does not stop at the gate [5].

Two things follow from drawing it this way. Earlier is better, because every intercept a person passes means more time in custody. And no single agency owns the path. Communities use the model, in its own words, to "identify gaps, resources, and opportunities at each intercept" [5]. It is a map for finding out who should be in the room.

## How it works in Alabama

The justice system is a regular point of contact for Alabamians with mental illness. The director of the state's Crisis Intervention Team program estimates that 20 to 30 percent of the calls law enforcement fields involve a person in a mental health crisis [6]. Walk the six intercepts and a pattern appears. The state has built a good deal at the start of the path and is under court order further along it, and in between there is a stretch where very little is counted.

Intercept 0 is the crisis system: 988, the mobile teams, and six crisis centers that accept police drop-offs at any hour [12]. Intercept 1 belongs to the police and sheriffs. Crisis Intervention Team training, which prepares officers to handle these encounters, is coordinated by NAMI Alabama and has a $1 million line in the state budget [145] [18]. The Department reported more than 1,375 trained officers in July 2024, and no later count was found. The state's police standards commission requires 12 hours of continuing education a year and names no mental health subject among them [135] [143].

Intercept 2, the booking desk and the first court appearance, is the thinnest. No statewide standard for mental health screening at booking was found, and no statewide count of jail suicides. What exists is local. Morgan County's jail, to take the report's example, employs two licensed counselors [229]. Since October 2025 the former drug courts, renamed accountability courts, may take defendants with mental illness [63].

Intercept 3 is where the federal courts come in. Defendants whose competency to stand trial is in doubt wait in county jails for an evaluation or a state hospital bed, and a consent decree governs how long they may wait. In July 2026 there were 291 people on that list [32]. A separate primer covers it.

At Intercept 4, reentry, the tools belong to the Bureau of Pardons and Paroles, which runs five Day Reporting Centers with contracted mental health and substance use treatment [243]. Alabama law suspends a prisoner's Medicaid and does not terminate it, so that coverage can resume, though how often it does at release was not found [248]. Intercept 5 is supervision in the community. Thirty-eight community corrections programs cover 52 counties and fifteen counties have none, on a map dated April 2023 [249].

## How this report uses it

Section 10 takes the intercepts one at a time and then gathers them in a table that sets out, for each, what exists, what is measured and what is missing. Read the "published measure" column closely, because it shows how uneven the evidence is: a call volume here, a single county's example there [229].

The model also explains why the same service appears twice. The crisis centers of Domain 3 are Intercept 0 in Section 10 [12]. Domain 4 discusses officer training as a workforce matter, and here it is Intercept 1. If you work locally, use the model as it was meant to be used, as a list of who to invite: the sheriff, the probate judge, the community mental health center, the district attorney, the parole office. A 2025 law already gives the first two a place on the executive committees of the larger regional mental health boards, where they sit on the board [61].
