## What it is

It would be convenient if a shortage of mental health workers were a single number. It is at least four, collected by different bodies for different purposes, and they do not always agree. Two of them put Alabama in very different places [34] [35].

The first is the Health Professional Shortage Area, or HPSA. The federal health workforce agency designates these, and a designation "can be geographic areas, populations, or facilities" [308]. One designation may cover a whole county and another a single prison or clinic. The designation is a gateway. Clinicians who agree to work at an approved site in a shortage area for at least two years can have student loans repaid through the National Health Service Corps [139].

Attached to the designations is a second number, percent of need met. It is narrower than it sounds. The figure is the number of psychiatrists available in the designated areas divided by the number that would be needed to remove the designations, with the target set at one psychiatrist for every 30,000 people [34]. Only psychiatrists are counted. A county full of counselors and nurse practitioners and short of psychiatrists scores badly.

The third measure widens the lens. Mental Health America's workforce ratio divides a state's population by all of its mental health providers: "psychiatrists, psychologists, licensed clinical social workers, counselors, marriage and family therapists, and advanced practice nurses" [35]. The fourth comes from inside the system. It is the count of funded posts that employers cannot fill.

## How it works in Alabama

Alabama has 67 mental health shortage designations, and 2,934,202 people live in them, which is more than half the state's population of 5.19 million [34] [133]. The count happens to equal the number of counties. Don't read it that way, since a designation can be a population group or a facility as easily as an area [308].

Now the surprise. Alabama meets 28.4 percent of need in those areas, and the figure for the whole country, in the same federal data, is 27.3 percent [34]. On that measure Alabama looks unremarkable. Yet Mental Health America places the state 51st of 51 for workforce availability, with one provider for every 740 residents against one for every 320 nationally [35]. Both are right. Alabama's shortage areas are about as short of psychiatrists as shortage areas elsewhere, and the state has far fewer mental health professionals of all kinds, taken together, for its population. Anyone who quotes only the first number will understate the problem.

Neither figure says anything about the public system in particular. For that there is one data point. The Department reported in 2024 that its mental health provider network had 752 vacancies at the end of FY2023, against a need of 4,678 full-time positions [135]. No later statewide figure was found as of October 2026. The sharpest local case is the forensic hospital, where about half of technician and nursing positions were filled in August 2024 [136] [116].

Licensure compacts are the remedy most often mentioned, and they need the same careful reading. A compact lets a clinician licensed in one member state practice in another. Alabama has joined seven. The psychology compact has been in effect here since June 2021 and the nursing compact since January 2020 [48] [83]. The Counseling Compact is a different story: ten states were issuing privileges under it by 1 October 2026, and Alabama was not yet one of them [82]. The Social Work compact, which Alabama joined in 2024, had not begun issuing multistate licenses anywhere [87] [137]. Membership on paper adds no clinicians.

The longer-term remedy is training. The University of Alabama announced a psychiatry residency whose first class was to start in June 2026, with six residents a year [138], and a 2026 appropriation directs $2 million of opioid-settlement money to a psychiatry residency at UAB [92].

## How this report uses it

Domain 4 gives all four measures in its first two paragraphs, so read them side by side there. Domain 7 adds the rural dimension, and Domain 4 itself notes that child psychiatry is the specialty most often named as short. The report's own reading is that the compacts and telehealth ease the outpatient end of the system and do nothing for inpatient units, which need staff on the ward [34].

When a workforce number is quoted to you, ask which of the four it is, what year it describes and who is counted. The ranking is from data for 2024 [35]. The vacancy count is from 2023 [135]. And Domain 4 is candid that on turnover and burnout Alabama publishes almost nothing at all.
