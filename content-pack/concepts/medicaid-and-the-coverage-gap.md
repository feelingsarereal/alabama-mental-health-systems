## What it is

Medicaid is usually described as health insurance for the poor, and in most of the country that is close enough. Each state runs its own program inside federal rules, and Washington pays a share of every dollar spent. The share is called the federal match, or FMAP. It comes from a formula that gives "higher reimbursement to states with lower per capita incomes", with a floor of 50 percent and a ceiling of 83 [304]. Alabama's rate sits well up that range, at 72.63 percent for FY2026 [95]. So for each dollar of Medicaid care, the state has to find about 27 cents.

Who may enrol is the part a state controls, and this is where expectations go wrong. The Affordable Care Act let states open Medicaid to all adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty level [38]. Forty-one states, counting the District of Columbia, have done so. Alabama is one of the ten that have not [281].

The same law built the insurance marketplaces on the assumption that every state would expand. Premium subsidies were set to begin at the poverty line, because anyone poorer was supposed to have Medicaid. Then expansion became optional, and in KFF's words "poor adults living in states that decided not to expand were left without an affordable coverage option" [38]. That hole is the coverage gap. An adult just above the poverty line can buy subsidized insurance. One just below it cannot.

## How it works in Alabama

A parent in Alabama qualifies only with income up to about 18 percent of the poverty level [282], and an adult with no dependent children and no disability does not qualify at any income [283].

How many people are in the gap depends on who is counting. Alabama Arise, which campaigns for expansion, puts it at up to 155,000 [36]. KFF's estimate from 2024 survey data is 86,000 uninsured adults in the gap itself and 147,000 who would become eligible if the state expanded [38]. The larger KFF number reaches above the poverty line, to 138 percent, and so takes in people who can already buy subsidized marketplace plans. The two should not be averaged.

For mental health the consequence is direct. Medicaid is the largest single funder of the care the Department of Mental Health contracts for [16]. A service Medicaid pays for can grow. A person Medicaid does not cover has to be paid for from somewhere else, and that somewhere is mostly the state's General Fund. Among Alabama adults with any mental illness, 14.1 percent are uninsured, against 9.2 percent nationally [35].

When Alabama wants to change what its Medicaid program does, it has two instruments. A state plan amendment alters the standing agreement between the state and the federal government [305]. Mobile crisis teams became a Medicaid benefit this way in October 2023 [75]. A Section 1115 waiver is permission to do something the ordinary rules forbid. Waivers are approved as experiments, generally for five years, and must not cost the federal government more than it would otherwise have spent [306]. Alabama holds one. Medicaid has historically not paid for adults aged 21 to 64 in a psychiatric institution of more than 16 beds [307], and the waiver lifts that rule for facilities in Mobile and Baldwin counties until May 2027 [97]. A second waiver, for substance use treatment, has been pending with the federal Medicaid agency since July 2023 [98].

Unlike most states, Alabama does not hand Medicaid to private health plans. It has no comprehensive managed-care plans and pays providers itself [125], which is why the state could introduce the CCBHC daily rate by its own action.

The terms of the expansion debate shifted in 2025. A federal budget law ended, from January 2026, the temporary extra match that had been offered to states expanding for the first time [80]. The same law limits the provider taxes that states use to raise their own share, and were Alabama to expand, its 6 percent hospital tax would have to fall to 3.5 percent by FY2032 [81].

## How this report uses it

Section 12 has the full account, and Domain 2 places Medicaid among the Department's revenues. Domain 7 reads it as an equity question, and Section 10 asks what happens to a person's Medicaid on the way into jail and out again. Domain 1 says of coverage policy that it affects more sections of the report than any other single decision [36].

When you read about a new service, ask which payer it belongs to. If the answer is Medicaid, check whether it arrived by state plan amendment or by waiver, since a waiver comes with an end date. If the answer is the General Fund, you are probably looking at something Medicaid does not cover, or at care for people it does not reach [16].
