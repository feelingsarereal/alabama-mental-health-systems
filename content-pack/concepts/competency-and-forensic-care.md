## What it is

"Forensic" simply means connected with the courts. Forensic mental health care is care for people who are in the criminal justice system, and most of it concerns one question: is this defendant able to stand trial? In Alabama an office of the Department of Mental Health oversees it [232].

A trial is only fair if the accused can take part in it. Alabama's court rules say a defendant is incompetent if he or she "lacks sufficient present ability to assist in his or her defense by consulting with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" [299]. Notice the word "present". Competency is about the defendant's state of mind now, in the courtroom. Whether illness explains what the person did at the time of the alleged offense is a separate question, with a separate evaluation [232].

When there is enough doubt, the court orders an evaluation. If the judge then finds the defendant incompetent, and there is a substantial probability that treatment will change that within a reasonable time, the defendant is committed to the Department of Mental Health for what is called competency restoration [232]. Restoration is a legal goal. It aims to get the person well enough to understand the proceedings and work with a lawyer, which is not the same as recovery. Until it happens the case generally cannot move [32].

## How it works in Alabama

You might expect that a person a judge has found too ill for trial would leave jail for a hospital within days. In Alabama restoration has mostly meant a bed at Taylor Hardin Secure Medical Facility in Tuscaloosa, the state's forensic hospital, and there have not been enough beds or enough staff to run them. (The Department's own primer names Taylor Hardin for men and Bryce Hospital for women [232].) So people wait where they already are, which is the county jail. In 2025 the average wait was more than a year, and more than thirty people had waited over two [116].

A federal court is already supervising the problem. A class action now captioned *Hunter v. Boswell* was settled in 2018 with a consent decree, an agreement between the two sides that a judge has made binding [11]. As the Associated Press describes its terms, the state has 60 days to complete an evaluation and 30 more to admit the person for restoration [116]. The Alabama Disabilities Advocacy Program monitors compliance. In March 2025 the part of the decree covering inpatient restoration was extended to 31 January 2027 [11].

The list of people waiting stood at 327 in January 2026 and 291 in July, against a Department goal of 100 by the end of the year [32]. Taylor Hardin has grown from 140 beds to 225, about 200 of which were filled in August 2026. A telehealth program restored 32 people inside county jails between the summer of 2025 and August 2026. Staff called forensic navigators go through the list looking for people who might be better served outside the forensic system [32]. Beds are only useful with people to staff them, though, and in August 2024 the Commissioner told her board that about half of Taylor Hardin's technician and nursing positions were filled [136] [116].

Alabama has been here before. In *Wyatt v. Stickney*, decided in 1971, a federal court held that people confined in the state's psychiatric institutions had a constitutional right to adequate treatment, and the Department stayed under court supervision until 2003 [67] [68].

The other live case is about a different group altogether. *Braggs* concerns people serving sentences in state prisons, which are run by the Department of Corrections. In 2017 a federal judge found the prisons' mental health care "horrendously inadequate" and in violation of the Eighth Amendment [71] [69]. On 24 June 2026 the appeals court upheld that finding and the system-wide injunction. It reversed a specific staffing mandate and some requirements for suicide-resistant cells, and sent those questions back to the trial court [69] [70].

## How this report uses it

Section 10 has the fullest account, under Intercept 3 for *Hunter* and Intercept 4 for *Braggs*. Domain 3 gives the hospital figures, and Domain 4 explains why staffing at Taylor Hardin decides how long people sit in jail. Domain 1 makes the wider point that court orders are still doing work that policy has not done [11] [69].

As you read, keep the measures apart. The waitlist is a count of people on a given day [32], while the decree's deadlines are measured in days per person [116]. Taylor Hardin's bed count is capacity, and capacity matters only when the beds are staffed and open [136]. January 2027 is the date to watch.
