## What it is

Ask most people to describe a state's mental health system and they will start drawing an organization chart, with the department at the top and its hospitals and offices underneath. The World Health Organization starts somewhere else. Its 2007 framework set out "to promote a common understanding of what a health system is", and the answer it gave was a list of jobs that any health system has to do, whoever happens to do them [300].

There are six. WHO calls them building blocks: service delivery, the health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership and governance [1]. The scheme does not say which agency owns which job, and that is why it is useful. A state can be checked block by block. Is anyone governing? Is the money there, and whose money is it? Are there enough trained people to do the work? The blocks also lean on each other, so a clinic can be fully funded and still sit half empty for want of staff.

This report takes the six and adds two of its own. Equity asks who the system reaches and who it misses. Community engagement asks how the public shapes it. Neither is among WHO's six [1]. Together they make the eight domains of Part II. Four more sections follow in Part III, on children and families, the justice system, housing and social conditions, and payers, because a good deal of what decides a person's care in Alabama happens outside the Department of Mental Health.

## How it works in Alabama

A map of functions suits Alabama because responsibility here is scattered. The Department of Mental Health is the state mental health authority [51]. But the Alabama Medicaid Agency sets the covered services and rates that determine what providers can bill [49], probate courts decide civil commitments [66], and the Legislature writes the appropriations and, in its 2026 session, attached conditions to them [17]. An organization chart of the Department would leave all of that off the page.

Follow one thing through the report and the design shows itself. The six crisis centers appear in Domain 3 as a service, a place that takes walk-ins and police drop-offs around the clock [12]. In Domain 2 they are a $36 million line in the General Fund [18]. Domain 5 treats them as buildings, with capital costs the state evaluator put at between $3.3 million and $12.6 million a center. Domain 6 treats them as a measurement problem, since the same evaluator could not verify the centers' diversion counts [14]. Section 10 meets them once more, as the earliest point at which someone can be steered away from jail. Five appearances, five different questions. The repetition is deliberate.

Each domain is also held up against two yardsticks. One is the continuum of care: a short "Continuum lens" passage asks where, along the line from prevention to recovery, the domain is strong or thin [3]. The other is the state's own plan. The Department's Strategic Plan 2025–2028 has five priorities [40], and each domain closes with a line listing the plan goals it touches. Treat those codes, A1 to E3, with some care. They are Little Orange Fish's numbering, taken from its own analysis of the full plan document, and the Department's public page shows only the five priority headings  [40].

## How this report uses it

Use the domains as an index of questions. If you want to know who decides something, start in Domain 1. If you want to know why something already decided has not happened, the answer is usually in Domain 2 or Domain 4. Domain 4 says of itself that every other domain depends on it, and the forensic hospital shows why: in August 2024 only about half of its technician and nursing positions were filled [116] [136].

The four sections of Part III each borrow a frame of their own, and each frame has a primer on this site. Section 9 reads children's services against the System of Care principles [7], and Section 11 uses the five Healthy People 2030 domains [8]. Section 10 walks through the Sequential Intercept Model [4]. Section 12, on payers, is where the coverage questions raised in several domains are worked out in full. One caution applies throughout: the "Gaps and opportunities" bullets that close each domain are the report's own reading of the evidence, and should be quoted as analysis and not as the finding of any agency.
