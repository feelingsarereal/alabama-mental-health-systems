## What it is

Civil commitment is treatment ordered by a court for a person who has not agreed to it and has not been charged with a crime. Most people imagine it as a medical decision: a doctor in an emergency room signs a form and the patient is admitted. In Alabama it is a court case, heard by a probate judge [66].

It begins with a petition. Any individual may file one, in the probate court of the county where the person is [66]. Probate courts are the county courts most people know from wills and estates. The petition has to be sworn, and it has to describe the specific behavior that makes the petitioner believe the person is mentally ill [66].

The judge then appoints a guardian ad litem to protect the rights of the person named, who is called the respondent [297]. If the petition asks for the respondent to be held before the hearing, the judge may allow that only to prevent a real and present threat of harm or to stop the person leaving the court's reach. Even then the law is firm on one point: "No respondent shall be placed in a jail or other facility for individuals accused of or convicted of committing crimes" [297]. A probable-cause hearing must follow within seven days, and the final hearing within 30 days of the petition being served [297].

At the final hearing the test is strict. The judge needs clear and convincing evidence, a standard that sits between the ordinary civil one and the criminal one. For an inpatient order four things must be shown. The person has a mental illness. Because of it he or she poses a real and present threat of substantial harm to self or others. Without treatment the person will go on deteriorating. And the person cannot make a rational decision about treatment [66].

## How it works in Alabama

Once the test is met, the judge does not have a free hand. The statute says the order must be for the least restrictive alternative that is "necessary and available" [66]. That can mean outpatient commitment, where the person lives at home under an order to accept treatment. The outpatient test is different from the inpatient one. It does not require a threat of harm. The judge has to find that the person will deteriorate without treatment and "is unable to maintain consistent engagement with outpatient treatment on a voluntary basis" [298]. There is a catch. Outpatient treatment can be ordered only if the designated facility has consented to provide it [66].

Either kind of order lasts at most 150 days. A renewal can run for up to a year [66].

The word "available" is where the law meets the budget. An inpatient order sends the person to the Department of Mental Health, for a bed in a state hospital or in a facility the Department designates [66]. In January 2026 the Department counted 771 such beds: 285 at Bryce and Harper hospitals, 234 contracted in community hospitals, and 252 crisis residential beds at certified providers. That is 15.1 beds per 100,000 residents. The benchmark the Department uses, taken from the Treatment Advocacy Center, is 30 per 100,000, or 1,550 beds [30]. An order does not create a bed, and the statute says as much: nothing in it requires "a mental health provider to expand his or her current services if necessary funding is not provided" [297].

So "civil-commitment beds" has become a budget line. The FY2027 General Fund carries $8 million for civil commitment and residential services and a further $2.5 million for additional beds [18]. The Department prices closing the 779-bed gap at $226.4 million as a five-year goal [30]. One number is missing. Alabama publishes how many beds it has for committed people, but no annual count of commitments was found in the Department's reports as of October 2026.

## How this report uses it

Domain 1 sets out the law in a paragraph, and Section 10 returns to it at Intercept 2, where a probate petition is one of the routes by which a person in crisis ends up before a judge. Domain 3 and Domain 5 carry the bed counts. When those domains say "involuntary beds" or "civil beds", they mean beds that can receive a person under a probate order, as distinct from the forensic beds at Taylor Hardin, which serve the criminal courts [30] [32].

Probate judges appear elsewhere in the report as partners of the mental health system. A 2025 law gives a probate judge a place on the executive committee of the larger regional boards on which one serves [61]. For a local advocate that matters, because the county probate court is where commitment orders are made and the state publishes no count of them.
