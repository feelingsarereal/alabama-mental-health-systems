## What it is

When a state's mental health system makes the news, the story is usually about beds. There are too few of them, people wait in emergency rooms and jails, and replacing what was lost would cost a fortune. The continuum of care is a correction to that picture, and it comes from an unexpected quarter. In 2017 the national association of state mental health directors published a paper called *Beyond Beds* with the Treatment Advocacy Center, the same group whose minimum of 30 beds per 100,000 residents Alabama uses as its planning benchmark [30]. The paper's argument fits in a sentence: "24/7 inpatient care represents only a single component of a well-functioning continuum of care for any life-threatening health condition" [3].

A continuum is a line of services ordered by intensity. The National Academies' version begins before anyone is ill, with promotion aimed at everybody and prevention aimed at people at risk. It then runs through treatment to what it calls maintenance, meaning long-term care and rehabilitation [2]. This report marks five stops along the line: promotion and prevention, outpatient treatment, crisis response, inpatient and forensic care, and recovery supports such as housing and work.

Two tests come with the picture, and the report borrows both. Does each level exist at enough scale? And do the hand-offs between levels work [3]? The second test is easy to forget. Nobody stays at one level. A person stabilized in hospital needs a clinic appointment the following week, perhaps a supervised place to live for some months, and in time a job. Take away the next rung and that person either stays put, in a bed someone else is waiting for, or leaves without support and comes back.

## The key idea in one picture

Draw five boxes in a row, left to right: "promotion and prevention", "outpatient treatment", "crisis response", "inpatient and forensic care", "recovery supports" [3]. Join each neighbouring pair with a two-headed arrow labelled "hand-off". Between "inpatient and forensic care" and "recovery supports", add a sixth box with a dashed outline, labelled "step-down: residential care, intensive outpatient, supported housing", and mark it "the middle". Under the crisis and inpatient boxes write "built up since 2020". Under the dashed box write "no statewide count of beds published". Under "recovery supports" write "259 in supported housing, 234 in supported employment, of 94,410 served (2025)" [42].

## How it works in Alabama

Alabama has spent the years since 2020 building the two parts of the line that deal with emergencies. The crisis system, which hardly existed before, now has 988 call centers, mobile teams and six crisis centers [13]. On the inpatient side the Department counted 771 involuntary beds in January 2026 and would like about twice that, to reach the 30-per-100,000 benchmark [30]. Those are the numbers legislators argue over.

The everyday level is outpatient care. It runs through the 19 community mental health centers, which are the way in for therapy, medication, case management and most other services [31] [108]. Further along, the line gets thin. In the 2025 reporting year 259 people were in supported housing and 234 in supported employment, out of 94,410 people the public system served. Assertive Community Treatment, in which a team looks after people who would otherwise be in and out of hospital, reached 897 [42].

This is what the report means when it says the middle is missing. "The middle" is not the centre of the diagram. It is everything that lies between a locked ward and a life of one's own: step-down residential care, intensive outpatient programs, supported housing. The Department does pay for a ladder of residential services through its contracts with the centers, but no statewide count of those beds was found in its published reports as of October 2026 [108]. So Alabama publishes its hospital beds to the last one and gives no figure at all for the places people go when they leave.

Money explains some of the pattern. In the report's reading, state dollars concentrate on crisis and inpatient care, while outpatient treatment and recovery supports depend on Medicaid, and so on whether a person has coverage [18]. A word of warning about vocabulary, too. Written with capitals, a "Continuum of Care" is a regional homelessness planning body recognized by the federal housing department, and Alabama has nine of them [260]. Section 11 uses the phrase in that sense only.

## How this report uses it

Domain 3 is the continuum set out service by service, so begin there. Then look for the short "Continuum lens" passage in each of the other domains, which puts the same question to money, staff, buildings and data in turn. Section 11 takes up housing and employment as systems in their own right. Section 12 explains why the new CCBHC payment, which pays one clinic for crisis care, outpatient treatment and follow-up together, is the report's main hope for mending the hand-offs [10] [105].

When you read a proposal for more beds or another crisis center, ask what the report asks. Where does the person go next, and who is paid to see that they get there? At present the second question has no numerical answer: no statewide measure of follow-up within seven days of discharge was found in the Department's reports or the federal tables as of October 2026 [42].
