## What it is

It is natural to think of mental health care as what happens in a clinic or a hospital. The two frameworks in this primer were both written to push against that habit. One concerns children [7]. The other concerns everybody [8].

The System of Care idea dates from the mid-1980s, when a federal program for children's mental health services began and the concept was first set down [7]. Its premise is that a child with serious emotional difficulties is seldom the business of one agency alone. So the framework asks for services "organized into a coordinated network", one that "builds meaningful partnerships with families and youth" [7]. Three values sit at the core. Care should be driven by the family and guided by the young person. It should be based in the community. And it should fit the family's culture and language [7].

Under those values come working principles: a broad array of services, a plan made for the individual child, links across the agencies that serve children, early identification, and help with the move into adulthood [7]. An update in 2021 kept all of these and added several more, among them trauma-informed care and the integration of mental health with physical health [210].

The second framework is wider. Healthy People 2030, the federal government's set of national health goals, defines social determinants of health as "the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age" [8]. It sorts them into five domains: economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context [8]. Income, housing and schooling shape a person's health alongside anything a doctor does.

## How it works in Alabama

Section 9 counts at least six public systems that deliver children's mental health care in Alabama: the Department of Mental Health, the schools, Medicaid, child welfare, juvenile justice and early-childhood education, with pediatric medicine alongside. That is exactly the situation the System of Care was designed for. The Department took a step toward it in October 2023, when it gathered its own children's programs into a single Office of Child and Family Services [212].

Hold Alabama against the principles and the results are mixed. On the array of services, the Department's providers offer crisis, outpatient, day and residential care for children with serious emotional disturbance, with at least one of these in every county [131]. Community mental health centers work inside 122 school systems [119]. On coordination the report found less. State law provides a Children's Policy Council and a State Team for children with multiple needs, and both have remits wider than mental health [79] [78]. No standing body dedicated to children's mental health was identified.

"Family-driven" has taken a particular form here. Since 2022 a school must have a parent's written opt-in before providing mental health services, at first for students under 14, and since October 2025 for students under 16 [121] [59]. Whether that has changed how many students are served is not known, because no before-and-after count was found. The state has also been without a youth survey since 2021 [171], which matters when Mental Health America ranks Alabama 50th of 51 on its youth measures [35].

A caution about the phrase. The Department's first strategic priority is also called "System of Care Transformation", and there it means the Department's service system as a whole [40].

For the social determinants, Section 11 goes through the five domains in turn, and housing is where the mental health system's own gap shows most clearly. Alabama's 2025 point-in-time count found 4,137 people homeless, 502 of whom were recorded as severely mentally ill [258]. In the same year 259 people were in supported housing through the public mental health system [42]. Local homelessness planning belongs to nine regional coalitions called Continuums of Care, which are funded through the federal housing department and sit outside the mental health system altogether [260] [262]. No state supportive-housing subsidy program was found as of October 2026.

## How this report uses it

Section 9 has a table that sets each System of Care principle beside what Alabama has. Notice which rows end with something not identified or not published [7]. Section 11 has a matching table for the five domains, giving the Alabama system, a published measure and a gap for each [8].

The two sections also explain choices made elsewhere in the report. Schools appear in Domain 3 as part of prevention and again in Section 9 as the largest point of contact with children [119]. When a problem seems to belong to no one, these two frameworks are usually the place to look.
